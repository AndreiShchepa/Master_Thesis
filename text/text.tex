In November 2025, an attacker exploited mathematical and authorization flaws in the decentralized finance (DeFi) protocol Balancer, draining approximately \$128 million\footnote{\url{https://www.rescana.com/post/comprehensive-analysis-of-the-128-million-balancer-v2-defi-exploit-attack-vectors-impact-and-mit}}. This incident, along with numerous subsequent exploits in DeFi protocols, highlights a fundamental challenge in blockchain development: once deployed, smart contracts are immutable, and vulnerabilities cannot be easily patched. Unlike traditional software where bugs might cause inconvenience or data corruption, smart contract vulnerabilities directly translate to financial losses, often measured in millions of dollars.

Current smart contract security is highly dependent on manual code audits supplemented by automated analysis tools. Professional audit firms employ expert security researchers who spend weeks examining contract code, looking for common vulnerability patterns, reasoning about edge cases, and trying to violate protocol invariants. While static analyzers and fuzz testers can detect well-known vulnerability classes with high automation, they struggle with the semantic gap between what code does and what it should do. Many critical vulnerabilities are not simple pattern matches, but rather logical flaws that emerge from complex interactions between contract components or violations of implicit business logic assumptions. This type of reasoning has historically required human expertise, making audits time-consuming and expensive~\cite{perez2021smart}.

Recent advances in Large Language Models have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in understanding and reasoning about code. Models trained on vast corpora of source code, such as GPT and Claude, can explain code functionality, identify potential bugs, and even generate secure implementations when properly asked. However, applying LLMs directly to smart contract security faces several challenges. First, LLMs have limited context windows that cannot process entire DeFi protocols with their complex interdependencies and imported libraries. Second, LLMs lack access to the structured analysis results produced by static analysis tools, which provide precise information about control flow, data dependencies, and detected patterns. Third, effective vulnerability analysis requires domain-specific knowledge about Solidity-specific pitfalls, common attack vectors, and audit best practices that may not be well-represented in general code training data. Finally, LLMs have a fixed knowledge cutoff: newly discovered vulnerability patterns and evolving best practices are absent from their parametric memory.

This thesis addresses these challenges by extending the existing Wake framework for security analysis of smart contracts with a Retrieval-Augmented Generation pipeline that grounds LLM reasoning in a continuously updated knowledge base of historical audit findings. The system constructs a domain-specific knowledge base from real-world vulnerability reports and exposes them to the LLM agent during its audit workflow. The goal is not to replace human auditors. Instead, it is to augment them with tools that automate information retrieval and pattern recognition, so that human expertise can focus on complex reasoning where it adds the most value.

The thesis is organized as follows. Chapter~1 introduces Ethereum, its execution model through the EVM and Solidity, and the common smart contract vulnerability patterns that appear in practical audits. Chapter~2 surveys the foundational code analysis techniques: static analysis, dynamic analysis, and formal verification, establishing the theoretical and practical basis for vulnerability detection. Chapter~3 traces the development of Large Language Models from sequence probability foundations through the transformer architecture, training paradigms, and prompting techniques, concluding with a review of their emerging applications in smart contract security. Chapter~4 describes the Wake framework, its internal model, analysis tools, and the existing Wake-AI module with its limitations. Chapter~5 presents the proposed methodology, describing its modules in detail and the idea behind it. Chapter~6 evaluates the system through different experiment suites, defining the best setup for the current implementation for the particular dataset. Finally, Chapter~7 concludes with a summary of contributions, limitations, and directions for future work.

\label{ch:ethereum}
This chapter introduces the foundations needed to understand Ethereum and its components, as this work is closely related to that platform. It first outlines the general principles of blockchain, then specializes in the Ethereum execution model through the Ethereum Virtual Machine (EVM) and the Solidity programming language. Finally, it summarizes common smart contract vulnerabilities that appear repeatedly in practical audits.


\section{Overview}
\label{sec:overview}
Ethereum, launched in 2015, marked a significant evolution in blockchain technology. First proposed by Vitalik Buterin in a 2013 white paper, Ethereum extends the ledger-centric model to a full application platform that supports smart contracts and decentralized applications (dApps) \cite{Buterin2013}. Rather than simply recording value transfers, it enables general-purpose programmable logic through the EVM while preserving the core properties of transparency, decentralization, and immutability \cite{wood2014yellow}. In effect, Ethereum transformed the blockchain as a generalized state-transition machine, broadening its applicability well beyond simple currency use cases.


\subsection{Blockchain}
\label{subsec:blockchain}

To understand Ethereum's role as a programmable state-transition system, it is useful to begin with the blockchain foundation on which it operates. A blockchain is a public distributed ledger maintained by a network of peer-to-peer nodes rather than a single central authority. It grows as an ordered sequence of blocks, beginning with an initial block (commonly called the \emph{genesis block}), where each subsequent block contains a complete list of transaction records and a cryptographic link to its predecessor. As additional blocks are appended, reversing or altering earlier transactions becomes computationally infeasible. Participants interact through addresses instead of real-world identities, enabling transparency while preserving pseudonymity. Because every node stores a copy of the chain and the full transaction history is publicly verifiable, the system provides strong transparency: value flows can be traced, state transitions inspected, and the integrity of past operations independently confirmed. In short, from the initial genesis block through every appended block, the blockchain architecture combines decentralization, tamper-resistance, and verifiable history in a single framework \cite{blockchain}.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{images/block_eth.png}
    \caption{Structure of an Ethereum blockchain node. Adapted from~\cite{blockchain_diagram_eth}.}
    \label{fig:block_eth}
\end{figure}

As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:block_eth}, a blockchain block links to its predecessor and successor by storing the cryptographic hash of the previous block's header within its own header, forming an immutable and ordered chain. Beyond this backward pointer, the block header contains four key fields: the \emph{previous block hash}, a \emph{timestamp} recording when the block was produced, a \emph{nonce} --- a 4-byte field adjusted during the consensus process, and the \emph{Merkle root}, a single hash that compactly summarizes all transactions contained in the block \cite{blockchain_diagram_eth}.

A \emph{transaction} is the fundamental unit of state change in a blockchain: a cryptographically signed instruction issued by a participant that records the transfer of value or data between addresses. Every user in the network possesses a key pair: a private key, which must be kept confidential, and a corresponding public key that serves as the user's verifiable identity. When a user wishes to issue a transaction, the process follows two phases. In the signing phase, the sender encrypts the transaction data with their private key, producing a digital signature that is broadcast alongside the original payload. In the verification phase, any recipient or node can validate the signature using the sender's public key, confirming both the authenticity of the sender and that the data has not been tampered with in transit. The algorithm most widely adopted for this purpose in blockchain systems is the Elliptic Curve Digital Signature Algorithm (ECDSA)\,\footnote{\url{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptic_Curve_Digital_Signature_Algorithm}}. Each signed transaction is broadcast to the network, validated by the nodes, and permanently written in a block, after which it becomes immutable.

\subsubsection*{Merkle Tree}

The lower portion of Figure~\ref{fig:block_eth} expands the block's transaction set into a \textit{Merkle tree}: a binary tree in which each leaf holds the cryptographic hash of an individual transaction and each internal node holds the hash of its two children's concatenation. Leaf hashes are combined pairwise and re-hashed level by level until a single Merkle root remains. The integrity guaranties of this construction rest on three properties of the underlying hash function: \emph{preimage resistance}, which makes it computationally infeasible to recover an input from its digest; \emph{second-preimage resistance}, which prevents an attacker from substituting a different transaction that produces the same leaf hash; and \emph{collision resistance}, which ensures that no two distinct inputs can be found sharing a hash value. Because every internal node is derived from its children through such a function, any alteration of a transaction propagates deterministically upward, changing the root and invalidating the block header, making the root a binding cryptographic commitment to the entire transaction set \cite{merkle1987, nakamoto2008}.

This design enables inclusion proofs of size $O(\log_2 n)$, where the amount of data blocks is denoted by $n$. To verify that a transaction belongs to a block, a verifier needs only the transaction itself, the header's Merkle root, and the minimal set of sibling hashes along the path from leaf to root. By contrast, a naive linear hash chain would force the verifier to re-hash every element sequentially, resulting in $O(n)$ verification cost. This logarithmic efficiency allows lightweight validation without downloading entire blocks, saving bandwidth and storage. Furthermore, the previous-hash pointer in each header secures global block ordering. Any retroactive modification breaks the chain link to all subsequent blocks, making tampering immediately detectable by any honest node in the network \cite{merkle_tree}.


\subsection{Consensus mechanisms}
\label{subsec:consensus}
Once the block structure is established, the network must agree on which block to append next. This process is governed by the consensus mechanism, the design of which depends on the blockchain trust model. Across different deployment models, two dominant consensus families have emerged.

\subsubsection*{Proof of Work}
Proof-of-Work (PoW) is the oldest and most widely adopted consensus mechanism in blockchain networks, first introduced by Nakamoto as the backbone of Bitcoin. The fundamental principle of PoW is straightforward: nodes in a peer-to-peer network, commonly referred to as \emph{miners}, compete to solve a computationally intensive cryptographic puzzle. This puzzle requires finding a nonce value $x$ such that the hash of the block header $y = f(x)$ satisfies a predefined difficulty target --- typically that the resulting hash begins with a certain number of leading zeros. The first miner to find a valid solution broadcasts it to the network, and upon verification by other nodes, the new block is appended to the chain. The successful miner is rewarded with newly minted cryptocurrency, incentivizing continued participation \cite{nakamoto2008, sabry2023}.

\begin{figure}[!h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{images/PoW.jpg}
    \caption{Proof of Work mechanism flow. Adapted from~\cite{pow_ledger}.}
    \label{fig:pow}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:pow} illustrates this process concretely: a puzzle is broadcast to three competing miners ($x_1$, $x_2$, $x_3$), each of whom independently computes a candidate solution. Only the submission from $x_2$ satisfies the difficulty condition, while those of $x_1$ and $x_3$ are rejected --- demonstrating the inherently competitive and probabilistic nature of block production under PoW.

Despite its security guaranties, PoW carries a significant drawback: the competitive puzzle-solving process demands enormous and continuously growing computational power. As the chain lengthens, information from past blocks feeds into each new cryptographic puzzle, making successive puzzles progressively harder and driving miners toward specialized hardware, from ordinary CPUs in Bitcoin's early days to purpose-built Application-Specific Integrated Circuits (ASICs) that cannot be repurposed once obsolete~\cite{SCHINCKUS2021111682}.

\subsubsection*{Proof of Stake}
Proof-of-Stake (PoS) emerged as a direct response to the energy and scalability limitations of PoW. Instead of competing through computational power, validators lock up (\textit{stake}) a quantity of the network's native cryptocurrency as collateral, and the probability of being selected to propose the next block is proportional to the size of that stake~\cite{nguyen2019pos}. This design replaces electricity with economic commitment as the primary security mechanism, yielding several distinctive properties:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Stake-weighted selection}. A validator's chance of proposing a block grows with the amount of cryptocurrency they have staked, aligning influence with economic exposure to the network.
    \item \textbf{Fee-based rewards}. Unlike PoW miners, who receive newly minted coins as block rewards, PoS validators are primarily compensated through transaction fees collected from the blocks they validate.
    \item \textbf{Energy efficiency at the cost of maturity}. PoS eliminates the need for specialized mining hardware and its associated electricity consumption, though the mechanism has a shorter track record and its long-term security guarantees are still being studied.
\end{itemize}

The most prominent real-world transition to PoS occurred on Ethereum. Under its original PoW design, the annual electricity consumption of the network was approximately 113~terawatt-hours, despite processing only about 15~transactions per second. On 15~September 2022, the Ethereum development community completed \textit{The Merge}~\cite{mit_merge}, a long-planned switch to PoS that proceeded without major disruption to the live network and reduced energy consumption by over 99\,\%\footnote{\url{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proof_of_stake}}.

Under Ethereum's current PoS implementation, a validator must deposit a minimum of 32~ETH into a dedicated smart contract. Block proposers are then selected pseudo-randomly and the remaining validators attest to each proposed block's validity. Finality is enforced by the Casper Friendly Finality Gadget (Casper-FFG): a block is considered final once two consecutive checkpoint blocks have been justified by a supermajority (2/3 of all staked ETH). Reverting a finalized block would require an attacker to burn at least one-third of the total staked supply, making such an attack economically devastating and practically infeasible~\cite{ethereum_pos, buterin2019casperfriendlyfinalitygadget}.

With a solid understanding of how blocks are structured and how consensus mechanisms work, we can turn our attention to the next layer of the architecture: how transactions and smart contract code are actually executed.

\section{Ethereum Virtual Machine}
\label{sec:evm}
The Ethereum Virtual Machine (EVM) is a deterministic state machine that executes the bytecode of the smart contract identically on all nodes. However, it is not Turing complete in the classical sense: execution is strictly resource-bounded by gas, so every instruction has a metered gas cost, and execution halts once the supplied gas is exhausted. 
This gas-bounded execution guaranties termination and prevents non-halting or deliberately expensive programs from stalling the network; accordingly, the EVM is often described as \textit{quasi–Turing complete} (computationally universal only under a finite gas budget)~\cite{antonopoulos2018mastering, wood2014yellow}.

\subsection{Gas}
Gas is the unit of account for the computational effort in Ethereum. Each EVM opcode has an associated gas cost, and a transaction specifies an upper bound (gas limit) on how much gas may be consumed during its execution.

Gas serves three core purposes: 
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{metering} computation and storage, so that the use of the resources is paid for by the initiator of a transaction;
    \item \emph{DoS resistance} and spam prevention, because excessively resource-intensive executions become economically infeasible;
    \item \emph{economic prioritization} of limited block space via fees.
\end{itemize}

Since \textit{EIP-1559}\,\footnote{\url{https://eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-1559}}, each block includes a dynamically adjusted \emph{base fee} that is burned and a user-specified \emph{priority fee} (tip) paid to the block proposer. 
Users submit \texttt{maxFeePerGas} and \texttt{maxPriorityFeePerGas}; the effective price paid per gas equals to \(\min(\texttt{maxFeePerGas}, \text{base fee} + \texttt{maxPriorityFeePerGas})\). 
Blocks have an elastic gas target and the base fee increases or decreases depending on recent block gas usage, stabilizing the fee market while preserving incentives.


\subsection{Architecture}

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{images/evm}
    \caption[Ethereum Virtual Machine illustration]{Ethereum Virtual Machine illustration. Adapted from \cite{evm_web}.}
    \label{fig:evm}
\end{figure}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Program Counter (PC) --- component that tracks the current instruction that it is executing.
    \item EVM code --- an immutable component holds the bytecode of the smart contract that is being executed.
    \item Account Storage --- part of the persistent state of the blockchain where the data of smart contracts are stored permanently. There are two types of account in the Ethereum environment:\texttt{ (i) }Externally Owned Account (EOA) that is owned by any external entity. Most often, it is referred to as a user account; and\texttt{ (ii) }Smart Contract Account that is owned by a smart contract and often controlled by an EOA that interacts with the deployed smart contract~\cite{Buterin2013}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Memory}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Stack} --- low-level memory structure that operates on a last-in, first-out (LIFO) principle. It stores small local variables and value types that are required for immediate execution. The stack is limited to 1024 elements, each 256 bits wide, and is accessible only through EVM opcodes or inline assembly. Careful management is essential, as exceeding its capacity causes the execution of the contract to fail \cite{evm_storage}.
    \item \textbf{Memory} --- dynamic and expandable storage area allocated for each function execution, which is then discarded once the function has finished. Unlike the stack, which is restricted to temporary data storage, memory allows for more flexible and longer-lived data retention.
    \item \textbf{Storage} --- persistent storage is associated with every smart contract and holds its state. Read and write operations in storage are relatively more expensive than those performed in memory. The data in storage are maintained as key-value pairs.
    \item \textbf{Calldata} --- read-only and immutable storage that holds function arguments and transaction data for the duration of a transaction call.
\end{itemize}

\section{Smart Contracts}
Building on the blockchain foundation introduced in Section~\ref{sec:overview}, Ethereum extends the ledger model with \emph{Smart Contracts}: programs stored on-chain and executed by all nodes under the same consensus rules. In practice, this means that the execution of the contract is deterministic and publicly verifiable: anyone can inspect the code, replay transactions, and validate the resulting state changes.

A contract is deployed through a transaction, receives its own on-chain address, and can then be called by externally owned accounts or by other contracts. This execution model reduces reliance on intermediaries and enables composable decentralized applications, where protocols interact as reusable building blocks.

\subsection{Solidity}
Solidity is Ethereum’s dominant high-level, statically typed, contract-oriented language, compiled into EVM bytecode. Several language concepts are especially important from a security perspective:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Visibility and interfaces}: (\texttt{public}, \texttt{external}, \texttt{internal}, \texttt{private}) define which functions are callable and from where.
    \item \textbf{Data locations}: (\texttt{storage}, \texttt{memory}, \texttt{calldata}) influence both correctness and gas usage.
    \item \textbf{External call primitives}: (\texttt{call}, \texttt{delegatecall}, \texttt{staticcall}) shape trust boundaries and control flow.
    \item \textbf{Failure semantics}: (\texttt{require}, \texttt{revert}, \texttt{assert}) determine how safely the contract handles unexpected states.
\end{itemize}

A common drawback of Solidity development is that subtle semantic errors can produce severe vulnerabilities. For example, performing an external call before updating the internal state or careless use of \texttt{delegatecall()} may execute foreign code in the caller’s storage context~\cite{soliditydocs}.

\begin{listing}[!h]
\begin{minted}[fontsize=\small,breaklines]{solidity}
contract Counter {
    uint256 public value;
    
    function inc() external {
        value += 1; // persistent on-chain state update
    }
}

\end{listing}

Even this trivial piece of code captures the core model, where a transaction triggers EVM execution, the state is updated, and the transition becomes part of the verifiable blockchain history.

\section{Security}

In Ethereum, security is not an add-on layer, but a property that must be deliberately engineered into every line of contract code. The EVM's execution model is transparent, deterministic, and adversarially accessible by design: any deployed contract can be inspected, called, and interacted with by anyone on the network. Unlike traditional software, deployed smart contracts are typically immutable, meaning that a discovered vulnerability cannot simply be patched with a software update. The financial stakes compound this risk: contracts routinely hold millions of dollars in assets, making them high-value targets that attract sophisticated adversaries. The following sections provide a structured treatment of some vulnerabilities.

\subsection{Common Vulnerabilities}
\label{subsec:common-vulnerabilities}

Smart contract vulnerabilities can be broadly classified into three categories based on their origin~\cite{atzei2017survey}:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Solidity-related} flaws arising from the design and semantics of the Solidity programming language itself;
    \item \textbf{EVM-related} issues rooted in the behavior and constraints of the Ethereum Virtual Machine;
    \item \textbf{Ethereum blockchain design-related} vulnerabilities that exploit properties of the underlying blockchain protocol, such as transaction ordering or block-level mechanics.
\end{itemize}

The rest of section illustrates the most popular attack patterns and minimal code examples. All vulnerable code snippets are provided strictly for educational purposes and must not be used in production.

\subsubsection*{Reentrancy attack}

This type of attack occurs when a contract makes an external call before it has finished updating its own state. The callee, which may be a contract under attacker control, can re-enter the caller inside the same transaction and observe or manipulate the intermediate, stale state. The classic pattern involves a \texttt{withdraw()} function that transfers Ether before reducing the caller's recorded balance.

\begin{listing}[!h]
\begin{minted}[fontsize=\small,breaklines]{solidity}
contract VulnerableVault {
    mapping(address => uint256) public balances;

    function deposit() external payable {
        balances[msg.sender] += msg.value;
    }

    function withdraw() external {
        uint256 amount = balances[msg.sender];
        require(amount > 0, "Nothing to withdraw");

        // (1) External call -- attacker's fallback fires here
        (bool ok, ) = msg.sender.call{value: amount}("");
        require(ok, "Transfer failed");

        // (2) State update happens AFTER the call -- too late
        balances[msg.sender] = 0;
    }
}
\end{minted}
\caption{Vulnerable vault contract susceptible to reentrancy.}
\label{lst:vulnerable-vault}
\end{listing}

An attacker deploys a malicious contract whose \texttt{receive()} fallback immediately calls \texttt{withdraw()} again. Because \texttt{balances[msg.sender]} has not yet been set to zero at step~(1), each recursive call passes the balance check, draining the vault until gas is exhausted.

The fix is straightforward: apply the \emph{checks-effects-interactions} pattern~---~update all internal states before making any external call. An additional defense is an explicit reentrancy guard \,\footnote{\url{https://docs.openzeppelin.com/contracts/3.x/api/utils\#ReentrancyGuard}}.

\newpage

\begin{listing}[!ht]
\begin{minted}[fontsize=\small,breaklines]{solidity}
contract Attacker {
    VulnerableVault public target;
    
    constructor(address _target) {
        target = VulnerableVault(_target);
    }
    
    function attack() external payable {
        target.deposit{value: msg.value}();
        target.withdraw();
    }
    // Re-enters withdraw() on every incoming Ether transfer
    receive() external payable {
        if (address(target).balance >= msg.value) {
            target.withdraw();
        }
    }
}
\end{minted}
\caption{Attacker contract exploiting the reentrancy vulnerability in Listing~\ref{lst:vulnerable-vault}.}
\label{lst:attacker-reentrancy}
\end{listing}

\subsubsection*{Denial of Service}

Denial of service (DoS) in Ethereum manifests itself as making a critical operation consistently revert or prohibitively expensive. One prevalent pattern involves iterating over an unbounded array to distribute funds or update state; an attacker who controls one entry in that array can cause the iteration to fail by forcing a revert and blocking all other participants.

\begin{listing}[!h]
\begin{minted}[fontsize=\small,breaklines]{solidity}
contract Distributor {
    address[] public recipients;
    
    function addRecipient(address r) external {
        recipients.push(r);
    }
    // An attacker adds a contract that always reverts on receive().
    // This causes the entire loop to revert, locking all funds.
    function distribute() external {
        for (uint256 i = 0; i < recipients.length; i++) {
            payable(recipients[i]).transfer(1 ether); // can revert!
        }
    }
}
\end{minted}
\caption{Distributor contract vulnerable to DoS via a reverting recipient.}
\label{lst:dos-distributor}
\end{listing}

The standard mitigation is a \emph{pull-payment} (withdrawal) pattern: instead of the contract pushing funds to all recipients in a loop, each recipient calls a \texttt{claimPayment()} function individually, so a malicious recipient can only block their own withdrawal.


\subsubsection*{Front-running}

Ethereum transactions are broadcast to a public mempool before they are included in a block. Block producers can observe pending transactions and exploit their ordering advantage. This enables \emph{front-running}: submitting a competing transaction with a higher gas price so that it is mined before the victim's, thereby profiting from the price impact the victim's transaction would have caused. Daian et al. formalized this phenomenon as the Miner Extractable Value (MEV). It is the total profit available from reordering, inserting, or censoring transactions within a block~\cite{daian2020flash}.

\begin{figure}[!h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{images/front-running.jpg}
    \caption{Mempool-based front-running. Adapted from~\cite{phemex2023frontrunning}.}
    \label{fig:front-running}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:front-running} illustrates how a sandwich attack exploits the mempool. Suppose a user submits a large buy order ($T_3$) on a decentralized exchange that will move the token price upward. An attacker observes $T_3$ in the mempool and submits two transactions: a buy ($T_4$) with a higher gas fee to ensure it lands \emph{before} the victim, and a sell ($T_2$) with a slightly lower fee to land \emph{after}. Since miners order transactions by gas price, the resulting block contains the attacker's buy first, then the victim's trade, and then the attacker's sell. The attacker buys at the lower pre-impact price, the victim's order pushes the price up, and the attacker sells at the inflated price, capturing the difference at the victim's expense. The victim's trade still executes, but at a worse price than expected.

Several mitigation strategies have been proposed. Commit-reveal schemes split a transaction into two phases: the user first submits a hashed commitment that hides the transaction details, and only reveals the actual parameters in a subsequent block, preventing attackers from reading and reacting to the contents in the mempool. Private transaction pools allow users to submit transactions directly to block builders rather than broadcasting them to the public mempool, removing the visibility that front-runners rely on. At the application level, decentralized exchanges can enforce maximum slippage tolerances, causing the transaction to revert if the price moves beyond an acceptable threshold between submission and execution. Although none of these approaches eliminate front-running entirely, they significantly raise the cost and complexity of carrying out such attacks.

\bigskip

The preceding discussion establishes that well-known smart contract vulnerabilities are well-defined and follow certain patterns, respectively. Manual code review, while indispensable, does not scale to the volume and pace at which new contracts are deployed. Even experienced auditors can miss subtle instances of a vulnerability buried in complex inheritance hierarchies or proxy patterns. This is precisely where static analysis can provide systematic, scalable assistance. 


\chapter{Code Analysis}
\label{ch:code-analysis}

The security analysis of software systems, particularly smart contracts deployed on blockchain platforms, relies on a diverse set of analytical techniques that have evolved over decades of programming language research. This chapter provides a comprehensive overview of the foundational methods for code analysis, establishing the theoretical and practical foundations necessary to understand different approaches for vulnerability detection. The discussion is organized around three complementary paradigms: static analysis, which reasons about program behavior without execution; dynamic analysis, which observes actual runtime behavior; and formal verification, which provides mathematical guaranties about program correctness.

\section{Static Analysis}
\label{sec:static-analysis}

Static analysis encompasses techniques that examine source code, bytecode, or other program representations to infer properties about all possible executions without actually running the program. The appeal of static analysis lies in its advantage of never missing a bug of the class it is designed to detect. However, this power comes at a well-known theoretical cost. \textit{Rice's theorem}\footnote{\url{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rice's_theorem}} establishes that any non-trivial semantic property of programs is undecidable in the general case, so every practical static analyzer must approximate. The consequence is that tools may report \textit{false positives}, flagging code as potentially buggy when no real vulnerability exists. Tuning this trade-off between soundness and precision is a central design challenge in every static analysis tool~\cite{nielson1999principles, rice1953classes}.

The rest of section decomposes the standard pipeline through which static analyzers process smart contract code: parsing it into a syntax tree, lowering it to an intermediate representation, and then applying control flow and data flow analyzes to reason about the program's behavior.

\subsection{Abstract Syntax Tree}
\label{subsec:ast}

The abstract syntax tree (AST) is the first structured representation that an analyzer builds from raw source code. It is a hierarchical tree whose nodes correspond to syntactic constructs, while abstracting semantically irrelevant details such as whitespace, comments, and parentheses~\cite{aho2006compilers}. The Solidity compiler itself produces a JSON-formatted AST that downstream tools can consume directly.

\begin{figure}[!h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=6cm, height=7cm]{images/ast.pdf}
    \caption{Abstract Syntax Tree example.}
    \label{fig:ast_diagram}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:ast_diagram} illustrates the structure of the AST. The root node represents the assignment operator, and each interior node corresponds to an operation, while the leaves hold the identifiers and operands. Operator precedence and the nesting of the function call are implicitly captured by the tree structure rather than by explicit parentheses.

However, as mentioned, the AST captures only the syntactic structure. It cannot directly represent the flow of data through a program or the paths that execution might take at runtime. Two syntactically similar code fragments may behave entirely differently depending on variable values and branching outcomes. These limitations motivate the use of semantically richer representations.

\subsection{Intermediate Representation}
\label{subsec:ir}

Intermediate representation (IR) bridges the gap between source-level syntax and low-level execution semantics by normalizing various syntactic constructs into a uniform format amenable to analysis. A well-designed IR removes syntactic sugar, resolves implicit type conversions, and exposes the underlying computational structure.

A particularly important technique in modern IR design is the Static Single Assignment (SSA) form, in which every variable is assigned exactly once and special $\phi$-functions are inserted at control-flow merge points to reconcile multiple reaching definitions. SSA simplifies many analyzes, such as constant propagation, dead code elimination, and use-def chain construction, because each use of a  variable points unambiguously to a single definition~\cite{rosen1988global}.

\subsection{Control Flow Analysis}
\label{subsec:control-flow}

The AST and IR represent \emph{what} a program computes, but not \emph{how} execution proceeds through it. Two programs with identical syntax trees can follow entirely different paths at runtime depending on branching conditions and loop bounds.

Control flow analysis determines the possible execution paths through a program, typically encoding them as a Control Flow Graph (CFG). In a CFG, each node represents a block, a maximal sequence of instructions without internal branching, and directed edges represent possible transfers of control between blocks. The CFG provides the structural backbone upon which most subsequent analyzes operate: loop detection, dominance computation, and path-sensitive reasoning all depend on it. It uses for reachability analysis, that determines which program points can actually be reached from a given entry.

At the inter-procedural level, the call graph extends this view by capturing the calling relationships between functions. Constructing an accurate control-flow graph from EVM bytecode is particularly challenging because, unlike conventional architectures, the EVM does not encode jump destinations directly in the instruction: instead, \texttt{JUMP} and \texttt{JUMPI} read their target from the execution stack, meaning that resolving where a jump leads may require tracking the stack state across the entire program. Solidity-specific features such as inheritance hierarchies, function modifiers, and low-level \texttt{delegatecall()} instructions further complicate matters, as the target of an invocation may be ambiguous or determined only at runtime~\cite{contro2023ethersolve}.

\subsection{Data Flow Analysis}
\label{subsec:data-flow}

Where control flow analysis maps the structure of possible executions, data flow analysis tracks how values propagate through that structure. 

Data flow analysis in Solidity faces a domain-specific complication: \textit{storage aliasing}. Solidity maps state variables to 256-bit storage slots according to packing rules that depend on type size and declaration order, while dynamic structures like mappings and arrays compute their slot addresses via hashing. Therefore, two syntactically distinct references may alias to the same physical slot, creating data flow paths that a naive analyzer will miss. Handling this correctly requires modeling Solidity's storage layout and, in sophisticated tools, incorporating alias analysis. As an illustration, consider the following vulnerable \texttt{withdraw()} function, which performs an external call before updating the caller's balance --- a classic reentrancy pattern:

\begin{listing}[!h]
\begin{minted}[fontsize=\small,breaklines]{solidity}
function withdraw(uint _amount) public {
  if(balances[msg.sender] >= _amount) {
    (bool result,) = msg.sender.call{value:_amount}("");
    if(result) {
      _amount;
    }
    balances[msg.sender] -= _amount;
  }
}
\end{minted}
\caption{Withdraw function vulnerable to reentrancy.}
\label{lst:withdraw-reentrancy}
\end{listing}

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=13cm, height=6cm]{images/cfg_ddg.pdf}
    \caption{Comparison between Control Flow and Data Dependency graphs.}
    \label{fig:cfg_ddg_diagram}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:cfg_ddg_diagram} shows both graphs derived from Listing~\ref{lst:withdraw-reentrancy}. In the CFG, the external call and the balance update appear on separate branches that both follow the guard condition, making the ordering between them explicit. The DDG reveals that \texttt{balances[msg.sender]} is read (in the guard) and written (after the call), highlighting a potential reentrancy vulnerability.

Despite its strengths, static analysis is inherently limited to reasoning about what a program \textit{might} do, not what it \textit{actually} does when executed against concrete input. False positives accumulate as approximations compound across layers of analysis, and a recent large-scale evaluation of eight static application security testing tools for Solidity found that even the best-performing tools struggle to balance recall and precision, with business-logic vulnerabilities and protocol-layer flaws largely eluding automated detection~\cite{li2024sast}. Furthermore, static analysis cannot capture behaviors that emerge only at runtime, such as interactions between multiple deployed contracts, oracle responses, or mempool-dependent transaction ordering.

These gaps motivate the complementary use of \textit{dynamic analysis}, which tests programs by executing them against concrete or symbolically generated inputs.

\section{Dynamic Analysis}
\label{sec:dynamic-analysis}

This perspective sacrifices soundness in exchange for precision, since every behavior it reports genuinely occurred rather than being a conservative approximation. In practice, the two approaches catch different classes of bugs, which is why strong security workflows typically combine both.

For smart contracts, dynamic analysis is especially valuable because it can exercise the \emph{stateful} nature of contracts: real vulnerability triggers often depend on specific sequences of transactions that leave the contract in an exploitable state, something that is difficult to reason about purely from source code.

% ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Fuzz Testing}
\label{subsec:fuzz-testing}

Fuzz testing (\emph{fuzzing}) is a dynamic testing technique that automatically generates inputs to drive a program through its execution paths, looking for crashes, assertion violations, or other anomalous behavior. The idea dates back to Miller~et~al. fed streams of random characters into UNIX utilities and discovered that roughly 24--33\,\% of them crashed. Since then, fuzzing has evolved from purely random input generation to a sophisticated family of techniques that incorporate coverage guidance, constraint solving, and domain-specific knowledge to systematically explore the state space of the program~\cite{miller1990empirical}.

Adapting fuzzing to smart contracts introduces a challenge not present in traditional software: \emph{statefulness}. A conventional fuzzer generates a single input per test case, but a smart contract's behavior depends on sequences of transactions that cumulatively modify persistent storage on the blockchain. Two calls to the same function can produce entirely different outcomes depending on the transactions that preceded them. Effective smart contract fuzzers must therefore generate and mutate transaction sequences rather than isolated inputs, substantially expanding the search space~\cite{choi2021smartian}. Some extensions exist to solve some weaknesses of fuzzing:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Coverage-guided}: after each execution, the fuzzer records which parts of code were visited and prioritizes inputs that reach new coverage. This approach systematically expands the explored portion of the program, steering the search away from repeatedly exercising the same paths.
    \item \textbf{Data-flow--aware}: tracks how values flow through the program at runtime and uses that information to guide mutation. The idea behind this is to help the fuzzer share successive inputs and make coverage more sufficient.
    \item \textbf{Hybrid} -- an equality check against a magic constant is astronomically unlikely to be satisfied by random bit flips. This extension of the naive method combines evolutionary fuzzing with symbolic execution to overcome such roadblocks. When the fuzzer stops at a hard-to-satisfy branch, the hybrid engine extracts the path constraint and invokes a Satisfiability Modulo Theories\footnote{\url{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satisfiability_modulo_theories}} (SMT) solver to produce a satisfying input, which is fed back into the mutation pool~\cite{torres2021confuzzius}.
    \item \textbf{Snapshot-based}: mitigates \emph{reexecution overhead} by capturing program state at interesting points and resuming directly from those checkpoints, avoiding redundant reexecution.
    \item \textbf{Invariant-based}: addresses this gap by letting the developer specify custom properties that should hold across all reachable states. The fuzzer then searches for input sequences that violate them. This idea originates from frameworks like QuickCheck for Haskell, which generates random function arguments and checks user-defined predicates, and has since been adapted to many domains~\cite{claessen2000quickcheck}. In Solidity, an invariant might assert that a token's total supply remains constant after any sequence of transfers; if the fuzzer discovers a call sequence that violates such a property, it reports the minimal triggering sequence as a counterexample.
\end{itemize}

% ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsubsection* {Limitations}
\label{subsubsec:fuzzing-limitations}

Regardless of the search strategy, the effectiveness of a fuzzing campaign is heavily influenced by the set of seed inputs from which the fuzzer generates new candidates. Techniques such as corpus distillation, which removes redundant seeds that do not contribute new coverage, and corpus scheduling, which prioritizes seeds likely to lead to novel discoveries, can substantially improve throughput.

Despite its practical effectiveness, fuzzing provides no completeness guaranty: if the fuzzer does not find a bug, this does not prove that the program is correct. Bugs that require very specific, unlikely input sequences or that depend on environmental conditions, which the fuzzer does not model, may be missed entirely. Coverage metrics offer some confidence that a large portion of the code has been used, but coverage is a necessary rather than a sufficient condition for bug detection.

These inherent limitations of dynamic analysis motivate the pursuit of \textit{formal verification}: techniques that aim to provide mathematical guaranties about program behavior across \emph{all} possible inputs, rather than merely the inputs explored during testing. Where fuzzing asks ``can I find an input that breaks this property?'', formal verification asks ``can I \emph{prove} that no such input exists?''.

\section{Formal Verification}
\label{sec:formal-verification}

Formal verification employs mathematical techniques to prove that programs satisfy specified properties with certainty, not just with high probability as testing provides. While static and dynamic analyzes may miss vulnerabilities or report false positives, formal verification can provide guaranties: if verification succeeds, the property provably holds for all reachable states; if it fails, a concrete counterexample demonstrates the violation.

However, formal verification faces significant practical challenges. The techniques are computationally expensive, often requiring substantial manual effort to specify properties and guide the verification process. Scalability limitations restrict application to smaller codebases or require abstraction that may introduce imprecision. Additionally, verification establishes conformance to a specification, but the specification itself may be incomplete or incorrect, leaving gaps in security coverage. As Tolmach~et~al. observed in their comprehensive survey, the vast majority of deployed smart contracts lack any kind of formal specification whatsoever, which presents a fundamental barrier to the widespread adoption of these techniques~\cite{tolmach2021survey}.

\begin{figure}[!h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=11cm, height=8cm]{images/fv_strategies_tradeoff.pdf}
    \caption{Trade-off between automation and expressiveness across the three principal formal verification strategies.}
    \label{fig:fv_strategies_tradeoff}
\end{figure}

A prerequisite for any formal verification effort is a rigorous formalization of the execution environment. In the Ethereum context, this means defining the semantics of the EVM. Unfortunately, this task is complicated by the fact that the canonical reference, the Yellow Paper~\cite{wood2014yellow}, is a semi-formal document that has historically contained ambiguities and under-specifications.

The subsections that follow examine three principal formal verification strategies: symbolic execution, model checking, and theorem proving. Each occupies a different point in the trade-off between automation and expressiveness, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:fv_strategies_tradeoff}.

\subsection{Symbolic Execution}
\label{subsec:symbolic-execution}

Symbolic execution runs programs with symbolic rather than concrete values, representing the program state as logical constraints over symbolic variables. Rather than computing specific outputs for specific inputs, it computes expressions over symbolic inputs that characterize the relationship between inputs and outputs. 

For vulnerability detection, this mechanism enables systematic exploration of program paths and automatic generation of inputs that trigger specific behaviors. Given a property to check, symbolic execution explores paths, collects the constraints under which the property would be violated, and queries a Satisfiability Modulo Theories (SMT) solver to determine whether satisfying inputs exist. If the solver finds a satisfying assignment, it provides a concrete exploit input; if the constraints are unsatisfiable, the property holds on that path~\cite{cadar2013symbolic}.

The principal limitation is the \emph{path explosion} problem: the number of execution paths grows exponentially with the number of branch points. Loops and recursive calls exacerbate this by potentially creating infinitely many paths. Practical implementations employ various strategies to manage this, e.g. bounded exploration that limits loop unrolling depth, state merging that combines similar symbolic states, or prioritized search heuristics that focus on the most promising regions of the state space.

When applied to EVM bytecode, symbolic execution must additionally model the blockchain execution environment: persistent storage, inter-contract message calls, and environmental variables such as block timestamps and transaction origins. This creates a tension between precision and soundness.

Consider a function with the assertion that the result of an increment should always exceed the input:

\begin{listing}[!h]
\begin{minted}[fontsize=\small,breaklines]{solidity}
function increment(uint256 a) public pure returns (uint256) {
    uint256 b = a + 1;
    assert(b > a);
    return b;
}
\end{minted}
\caption{Simple increment function with an overflow-checking assertion.}
\label{lst:increment-overflow}
\end{listing}

A unit test might call the function in Listing~\ref{lst:increment-overflow} with a handful of values and the assertion would pass every time. A symbolic execution engine takes a different approach: it treats $a$ not as a concrete number but as a symbol representing any possible \texttt{uint256} value. The engine computes $b$ symbolically as $b = (a + 1) \bmod 2^{256}$, since the arithmetic of the EVM is wrapped around it. At the \texttt{assert} statement, it constructs the negation of the assertion and passes it to an SMT solver:
%
\[
    (a + 1) \bmod 2^{256} \;\leq\; a
\]
%
The solver determines that this is satisfiable when $a = 2^{256} - 1$: adding~1 wraps to~0 and $0 \leq 2^{256} - 1$. It returns this value as a concrete counterexample, proving that the assertion can be violated. This example illustrates both the strength and the scope of symbolic execution: it is highly effective at discovering violations along individual execution paths, but it reasons one path at a time. Properties that must hold across all possible sequences of function calls require a technique that reasons about the entire state space. This is precisely what model checking provides.

\subsection{Model Checking}
\label{subsec:model-checking}

Model checking is another technique that exhaustively explores the state space of a system to determine whether it satisfies a given property, typically expressed in temporal logic. Unlike symbolic execution, which reasons about individual execution paths, model checking reasons about the entire transition system of a program, making it well-suited for verifying properties that span multiple invocations or depend on the ordering of events.

Two principal algorithmic strategies underlie modern model checkers:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bounded Model Checking} (BMC) unrolls the transition relation up to a finite depth $k$ and encodes the resulting computation as a satisfiability problem: if a property violation exists within $k$ steps, the solver finds it; otherwise, the property holds up to that bound;
    \item \textbf{Constrained Horn Clauses} (CHC) take a different approach, constructing \emph{function summaries} --- logical abstractions that capture a function's input-output relationship without inlining its body.
\end{itemize}

Despite its strength, model checking inherits the fundamental state explosion problem from the classical setting. Smart contract storage is effectively unbounded, because mappings can hold entries for any 256-bit key. That is why the state space of the interacting contracts grows combinatorially. Abstraction techniques, such as replacing concrete addresses with symbolic representatives or bounding the set of interacting contracts, are essential for practical scalability, but introduce a gap between the verified model and the actual deployed system that must be carefully assessed~\cite{tolmach2021survey}.

\subsection{Theorem Proving}
\label{subsec:theorem-proving}

Theorem proving represents the most powerful and most demanding approach to formal verification. Rather than exhaustively exploring a state space, theorem proving directly constructs mathematical proofs that programs satisfy their specifications. The expressiveness of theorem proving is unmatched: any property that can be expressed mathematically can, in principle, be proven. This includes properties beyond the reach of model checking and symbolic execution, such as correctness relative to complex mathematical specifications. Early work by the Ethereum Foundation produced a formal EVM definition covering all instructions, validated against over 40{,}000 official tests, and used it within a proof assistant to prove safety properties of deployed contracts, demonstrating end-to-end theorem proving from bytecode semantics to contract-level guarantees~\cite{hirai2017defining}.

However, the cost is substantial. Proofs require significant human expertise to construct, often demanding person-months of effort for non-trivial systems. The development process requires not just programming skill, but mathematical sophistication and deep familiarity with the proof assistant's logic and tactic language. For most development contexts, where time-to-market pressure is intense and code is frequently updated, full theorem proving of the entire codebase remains impractical.

\label{subsec:summary}

This chapter has surveyed the foundational techniques for program analysis. Static analysis, operating on representations from abstract syntax trees to inter-procedural data flow, provides scalable vulnerability detection with soundness guaranties but may produce false positives. Dynamic analysis, through transaction tracing and fuzz testing, observes actual execution behaviors with precision but cannot guaranty the absence of vulnerabilities. Formal verification offers mathematical certainty, but at a significant computational and human cost.

The previous chapters examined techniques for analyzing smart contract code through structural and mathematical lenses. Each technique operates on the syntactic and semantic properties of the code itself, and each has characteristic blind spots. This chapter turns to a fundamentally different class of approaches built on Large Language Models (LLMs), deep neural networks that learn to predict and generate text by training on massive corpora and, in doing so, acquire rich internal representations of syntax, semantics, and multi-step reasoning.
 
The chapter traces the ideas behind these models from first principles. It begins with the mathematical foundations of sequence probability and the limitations of early neural approaches. \section{Sequence Probabilities}
\label{sec:seq-prob}

Let $\mathbf{x} = (x_1, x_2, \dots, x_T)$ be a sequence of tokens --- these could be words in a sentence, symbols in a formula, or any discrete units drawn from a finite vocabulary. A language model assigns a probability to the whole sequence by factoring it with the chain rule of probability:

\begin{equation}
    P(\mathbf{x}) \;=\; \prod_{t=1}^{T} 
    P\!\bigl(x_t \mid x_1, \dots, x_{t-1}\bigr).
    \label{eq:chain-rule}
\end{equation}

Each factor on the right-hand side determines the likelihood of the next token given everything that preceded it~\cite{jm3}. Classical $n$-gram models approximate this by truncating the history to a fixed window of $n{-}1$ tokens, an assumption that works acceptably for short phrases but collapses when the prediction depends on context dozens or hundreds of tokens back.

The quality of a language model is measured by how well its predicted distribution matches the tokens that actually occur. The standard measure is the \textit{cross-entropy} --- the average negative log-probability assigned to the true next token:

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L} \;=\; -\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} 
    \log P_\theta\,\bigl(x_t \mid x_1, \dots, x_{t-1}\bigr),
    \label{eq:nll}
\end{equation}

where $\theta$ denotes the model's parameters. A related and more interpretable quantity is \textit{perplexity}, defined as:

\begin{equation}
    \text{PPL} \;=\; \exp(\mathcal{L}).
    \label{eq:perplexity}
\end{equation}

Perplexity can be read as the effective number of tokens the model is choosing between at each step: a model with perplexity 20 is, on average, as uncertain as if it were picking uniformly from 20 equally likely options. The lower perplexity indicates a better model.

To move beyond the fixed-window limitation of $n$-gram models, the field turned to neural networks that could, in principle, condition on the entire preceding sequence~\cite{hindle2012naturalness}.

\subsection{Recurrent Neural Networks}
\label{subsec:rnn}

Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) offered the first neural solution to variable-length conditioning. At each time step $t$, an RNN updates a hidden state that summarizes the entire prefix:

\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{h}_t = f\!\bigl(\mathbf{W}_h \mathbf{h}_{t-1} + \mathbf{W}_x \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}\bigr),
    \label{eq:rnn}
\end{equation}

where $f$ is a nonlinearity such as $\tanh$ or ReLU\footnote{\url{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rectified_linear_unit}}, and $\mathbf{W}_h$, $\mathbf{W}_x$ are learned weight matrices. The conditional distribution for the next token is then read from $\mathbf{h}_t$ \cite{Goodfellow-et-al-2016}.

\begin{figure}[!h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=10cm, height=8cm]{images/RNN.pdf}
    \caption{Recurrent Neural Network architecture.}
    \label{fig:rnn_diagram}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:rnn_diagram} illustrates the three main layers of an RNN and the recurrent connection that feeds the hidden state from one time step to the next. This feedback loop allows the network to maintain a form of memory over previous inputs. Crucially, all recurrent connections share the same weight matrix, enabling the network to process sequences of arbitrary length.

In principle, the hidden state can carry information from arbitrarily far back. In practice, however, the repeated multiplication of weight matrices during backpropagation causes gradients to either shrink exponentially toward zero or grow without bound --- the \textit{vanishing} and \textit{exploding gradient} problems. Bengio et al. analyzed this phenomenon formally, showing that the influence of an input at the time step $\tau$ on the hidden state at a later time step $t$ decays exponentially with distance $t - \tau$. The consequence is that standard RNNs struggle to learn dependencies that span more than roughly 10--20 time steps, even though many meaningful patterns in language and other sequential data require much longer memory~\cite{279181}.

\subsubsection*{Long Short-Term Memory}

To address this problem, Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks were specifically designed~\cite{hochreiter1997long}. They introduce a dedicated \textit{cell state} that runs through the entire sequence like a conveyor belt, regulated by three learned gates: a \textit{forget gate} that decides what information to discard, an \textit{input gate} that controls what new information to store, and an \textit{output gate} that determines what part of the cell state to expose as the hidden state. By learning to open and close these gates, LSTMs can preserve information over hundreds of time steps.

Despite this advance, a fundamental bottleneck remained: the processing is strictly sequential. The token $t$ cannot be computed until the token $t{-}1$ has been processed. This sequential dependency has two consequences. First, it makes training on large corpora prohibitively slow, since parallelism across positions within a sequence is impossible. Second, information about an element early in the sequence must survive passage through every intermediate hidden state before it can influence the processing of a distant element, even if the two are directly related. Both limitations motivated the search for an architecture that could process all positions simultaneously while maintaining the ability to model long-range dependencies.


% ============================================================
\section{Transformers}
\label{sec:transformers}
% ============================================================

The transformer, introduced by Vaswani~et~al. in 2017, addressed both limitations of recurrent models in a single architectural innovation. It replaced the sequential hidden-state chain with a mechanism that lets every token attend directly to every other token --- \textit{self-attention}. The result is a model that can be trained in parallel across all positions and that provides short, direct gradient paths between any pair of tokens regardless of their distance in the sequence~\cite{vaswani2023attentionneed}.

\subsection{Tokenization}
\label{subsec:tokenization}
 
Before any computation can occur, the model must convert the raw text into a sequence of discrete tokens from a fixed vocabulary. Modern LLMs use \emph{subword tokenization}, most commonly Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE)\footnote{\url{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byte-pair_encoding}}, which was adapted for neural machine translation by Sennrich~et~al.~\cite{sennrich2016bpe}. BPE starts with a vocabulary of individual characters and iteratively merges the most frequent adjacent pair into a single token, repeating until a target vocabulary size is reached. The result is a vocabulary of variable-length subword units: common words like ``the'' remain single tokens, while rare or compound words are split into recognizable pieces.
 
For source code, tokenization has particular implications. Subword tokenizers trained predominantly on natural language corpora develop merge rules optimized for English words and common punctuation, not for programming language syntax. As a result, identifiers, hexadecimal  addresses, and numeric constants in Solidity are often split into fragments that carry little individual meaning, consuming context window budget without conveying proportional semantic information. This mismatch motivates training tokenizers on mixed code-and-text corpora, as is done by several code-specialized models.
 
Once tokenized, each token is mapped to a dense vector through a learned \emph{embedding table} --- a matrix $\mathbf{E} \in \mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{V}| \times d_{\text{model}}}$ where each row corresponds to one vocabulary entry. These vectors are the model's initial numerical representation of each token, and they are refined through the subsequent attention layers. These embedding vectors, not raw characters, serve as input to the transformer.

The output of tokenization is a sequence of integer indices drawn from a fixed vocabulary. These integers are what the model receives as input, but neural networks cannot operate on discrete indices directly. The next step is to convert them into continuous vectors that the transformer can process.

\subsection{Embeddings}
\label{subsec:embeddings}

An embedding maps each token index to a fixed-dimensional dense vector in a continuous space where arithmetic operations are meaningful. The model maintains a learned matrix where $|\mathcal{V}|$ is the vocabulary size and $d_{\text{model}}$ is the embedding dimension. To embed a token with index $i$, the model retrieves the $i$-th row of $\mathbf{E}$. These rows are not hand-designed; they are learned parameters, updated during training alongside all other weights. Over the course of pre-training on billions of tokens, the embedding space develops a geometric structure in which tokens with similar meaning or function occupy nearby regions. This idea was demonstrated at scale by \textit{Word2Vec}, which showed that word embeddings capture semantic relationships as vector arithmetic: the vector for ``king'' minus ``man'' plus ``woman'' produces a vector close to ``queen''~\cite{mikolov2013}.

In a transformer, the initial embedding is only the starting point. As the token's vector passes through successive attention layers, it is progressively enriched with contextual information from the surrounding sequence. The output of the final layer is a \emph{contextual embedding} that represents not just the token in isolation, but the token in the context of everything around it. This is a critical distinction: the word ``bank'' receives different final-layer representations in ``river bank'' and ``bank account'', even though it starts from the same row in the embedding table.

This contextual embedding mechanism is what makes pre-trained transformers so versatile. A model like BERT~\cite{devlin2019bert} can be used as a general-purpose feature extractor: feed in a passage of text, extract the final-layer embeddings, and use them for downstream tasks: classification, similarity search, clustering. With this foundation in place, we now turn to the overall transformer architecture and the attention mechanism at its core.


\subsection{Architecture Overview}
\label{subsec:transformer-arch}

\begin{figure}[!h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\linewidth]{images/transformer.png}
    \caption{The original encoder--decoder transformer architecture. Adapted from~\cite{vaswani2023attentionneed}.}
    \label{fig:transformer}
\end{figure}

The complete transformer architecture, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:transformer}, consists of two stacks: an encoder (left) and a decoder (right), each composed of $N$ identical layers. On the encoder side, each layer contains two sub-layers: a multi-head self-attention mechanism and a position-wise feed-forward network, both wrapped in residual connections and layer normalization. The encoder processes the entire input sequence in parallel and produces a sequence of contextual representations.

The decoder mirrors this structure but adds a third sub-layer: a cross-attention mechanism that attends to the encoder's output, allowing each generated token to draw on the full input representation. Critically, the decoder's self-attention is \textit{masked} --- each position can only attend to earlier positions, which enforces the autoregressive property: tokens are generated one at a time, left to right, with each prediction conditioned only on previously generated tokens.

At the bottom of both stacks, the input and output tokens pass through their respective embedding layers and receive positional encodings. At the top of the decoder, a linear projection followed by \textit{softmax} converts the final hidden states into a probability distribution over the vocabulary, from which the next token is selected.

In practice, most modern LLMs use only half of this design. Encoder-only architectures discard the decoder and use the encoder's contextual representations directly for tasks like classification and retrieval. Decoder-only architectures discard the encoder and generate text purely autoregressively. This is the variant used by the majority of today's frontier models. The remainder of this section examines the mechanisms that make all three variants work: self-attention and positional encoding.


\subsection{Self-Attention Mechanism}
\label{subsec:self-attention}

Self-attention is the core operation that gives the transformers their representational power. The central idea is to let each token in a sequence compute a weighted combination of all other tokens, where the weights are determined by learned compatibility functions. The vector representation of each token is projected into three vectors, \emph{query}~$\mathbf{q}$, \emph{key}~$\mathbf{k}$, and \emph{value}~$\mathbf{v}$, through learned linear maps:
 
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}^Q, \qquad
    \mathbf{K} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}^K, \qquad
    \mathbf{V} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}^V,
    \label{eq:qkv}
\end{equation}
 
where $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times d_{\text{model}}}$ is the matrix of token representations and $\mathbf{W}^Q, \mathbf{W}^K \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}} \times d_k}$, $\mathbf{W}^V \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}} \times d_v}$ are the projection matrices. The attention output is then computed as:
 
\begin{equation}
    \text{Attention}(\mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{K}, \mathbf{V})
    \;=\; \text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\!\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)\mathbf{V}.
    \label{eq:attention}
\end{equation}
 
The matrix $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\!\top} \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times T}$ contains the compatibility score of every query--key pair. Dividing by $\sqrt{d_k}$ prevents the dot products from growing with the embedding dimension, which would push softmax into regions where its gradients are vanishingly small~\cite{vaswani2023attentionneed}. After softmax normalization\footnote{\url{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Softmax_function}}, each row becomes a probability distribution over positions, and the output for each token is the corresponding weighted average of the value vectors.
 
An intuitive reading: the query at position~$t$ asks ``what do I need to know?'', the keys at all other positions advertise ``here is what I contain'', and the resulting attention weights decide how much each position contributes to the updated representation at~$t$. This mechanism allows the model to relate any two positions in a sequence with a single computational step, regardless of their distance.


\subsubsection*{Multi-Head Attention}

Rather than computing a single attention function, transformers run $h$ independent attention heads in parallel, each with its own set of projection matrices, and concatenate the results:
 
\begin{equation}
    \text{MultiHead}(\mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{K}, \mathbf{V})
    = \text{Concat}\!\bigl(\text{head}_1,\;\ldots,\;\text{head}_h\bigr)\,\mathbf{W}^O,
    \label{eq:multihead}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \text{where}\quad \text{head}_i = \text{Attention}\!\bigl(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}^Q_i,\;\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}^K_i,\;\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}^V_i\bigr).
    \label{eq:head-i}
\end{equation}
 
The motivation for multiple heads is that a single attention function can capture only one type of relationship at a time. By running several heads in parallel, the model can attend to different aspects of the input simultaneously. Empirical probing studies confirm that different heads do indeed specialize --- some track syntactic dependencies, others capture coreference or semantic similarity---even without explicit supervision~\cite{clark2019does, vig2019analyzing}. The output projection~$\mathbf{W}^O$ then combines these diverse perspectives into a single representation for each token.

\subsection{Positional Encoding}
\label{subsec:pos-enc}

Self-attention is \emph{permutation-equivariant}: if the input tokens are shuffled, the outputs are shuffled in exactly the same way, with no change in their values. Since token order carries critical semantic information, the model must be given explicit information about position. This is the role of \emph{positional encoding}, which is added to the token vectors before they enter the attention layers.
 
The original transformer used fixed sinusoidal functions to encode position~\cite{vaswani2023attentionneed}. For each position $\text{pos}$ and each dimension $i$ of the representation:
 
\begin{equation}
    \text{PE}_{(\text{pos},\, 2i)} = \sin\!\left(\frac{\text{pos}}{10000^{2i/d_{\text{model}}}}\right), \qquad
    \text{PE}_{(\text{pos},\, 2i+1)} = \cos\!\left(\frac{\text{pos}}{10000^{2i/d_{\text{model}}}}\right).
    \label{eq:sinusoidal-pe}
\end{equation}
 
This design has an elegant property: the positional encoding of any fixed offset $k$ can be expressed as a linear function of the encoding at position $\text{pos}$, which in principle allows the model to learn to attend to relative positions. However, sinusoidal encodings are fixed and cannot adapt to the data.

More recent models have moved to \emph{relative} positional encoding schemes. Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE) encodes position by rotating query and key vectors in the complex plane, so that the dot product between a query at position $m$ and a key at position $n$ depends only on their relative distance $m - n$~\cite{su2024roformer}. RoPE has become the dominant choice in modern decoder-only LLMs, including LLaMA~\cite{touvron2023llama} and its derivatives, because it combines the benefits of relative encoding with straightforward integration into the standard attention mechanism.

\section{Model Training}
\label{sec:training}
% ============================================================

A transformer architecture on its own is simply a parameterized function; it becomes useful only through training. Building a capable LLM involves three broad stages, each operating at a different scale and serving a different purpose. \emph{Pre-training} exposes the model to vast quantities of text to learn general-purpose representations. \emph{Fine-tuning} adapts these representations to specific tasks or desired behaviors. \emph{Prompting} shapes the model's output at inference time through instructions and examples, without modifying any parameters. The following subsections describe each stage in turn.


\subsection{Pre-training}
\label{subsec:pretraining}

Pre-training exposes the model to massive text corpora using self-supervised objectives that require no human labeling. For decoder-only models, the objective is \emph{next-token prediction} (causal language modeling): minimize the cross-entropy loss from equation~\eqref{eq:nll} over all positions and all documents in the corpus. For encoder-only models like BERT, the objective is \emph{masked language modeling}: randomly mask a fraction of tokens and train the model to recover them from bidirectional context.

The simplicity of these objectives belies the enormous computational cost. State-of-the-art models are trained on corpora containing trillions of tokens, using thousands of GPUs running continuously for weeks or months. These costs mean that pre-training a frontier dense model from scratch is feasible only for a handful of well-resourced organizations. For most practitioners and researchers, the practical path remains to start from an existing pre-trained model and adapt it through fine-tuning.


\subsection{Fine-tuning}
\label{subsec:finetuning}

Fine-tuning adapts a pre-trained model to specific downstream tasks by continuing training on a smaller, curated dataset. The most common modern approach is Supervised Fine-tuning (SFT) on instruction--response pairs, which teaches the model to follow user intent reliably. By exposing the model to examples of the desired input--output behavior, SFT can bias it toward structured output, improve adherence to constraints, and sharpen performance on domain-specific tasks.

Fine-tuning carries risks. Training on a narrow dataset can cause forgetting, where the model loses general capabilities acquired during pre-training. For example, a model fine-tuned exclusively on Solidity code might lose its ability to explain concepts in natural language. Overfitting is a related concern: a model fine-tuned on a small, homogeneous dataset may perform well on similar examples but generalize poorly to novel inputs.

\subsubsection*{Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning}

Full fine-tuning updates every parameter in the model, which for billion-parameter architectures requires substantial GPU memory and risks catastrophic forgetting. Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)~\cite{hu2021lora} offers an elegant alternative. Instead of updating a weight matrix $\mathbf{W}_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times k}$ directly, LoRA freezes it and adds a low-rank decomposition:

\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{W} = \mathbf{W}_0 + \Delta\mathbf{W} 
    = \mathbf{W}_0 + \mathbf{B}\mathbf{A},
    \label{eq:lora}
\end{equation}

where $\mathbf{B} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}$ and $\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times k}$ are small trainable matrices with rank $r \ll \min(d, k)$. Only $\mathbf{A}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ are updated during training; the original weights $\mathbf{W}_0$ remain frozen. For a typical transformer layer with $d = k = 4096$ and $r = 16$, this reduces the number of trainable parameters from $\sim$16.8~million to $\sim$131~thousand per matrix---a reduction of over 99\%. QLoRA~\cite{xu2023qalora} extends this further by storing the frozen $\mathbf{W}_0$ in 4-bit precision, making it possible to fine-tune models with tens of billions of parameters on a single consumer GPU.

    \includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{images/lora.pdf}
    \caption{LoRA injects a trainable low-rank path 
    $\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}$ alongside the frozen pre-trained weight 
    matrix $\mathbf{W}_0$. Only the small matrices $\mathbf{A}$ and

    \label{fig:lora}

These parameter-efficient methods make it practical to adapt large models to domain-specific tasks such as smart contract analysis. However, even a well-fine-tuned model may produce outputs that are technically correct but poorly structured, overly verbose, or misaligned with what a user actually needs. The next challenge is shaping the model's output.
 
\subsection{Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback}
\label{subsec:rlvr}

Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) addresses this gap by optimizing the model's output to match human judgments of quality~\cite{ouyang2022training}. The process involves three stages:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Reward model training.} Human annotators rank multiple model outputs for the same prompt. A separate reward model is trained on these preference pairs to predict which outputs humans would prefer.
 
    \item \textbf{Policy optimisation.} The language model is treated as a reinforcement learning agent whose ``action'' at each step is the next token. Using Proximal Policy Optimisation (PPO), the model is fine-tuned to maximise the reward model's score while staying close to the SFT policy via a KL-divergence penalty, which prevents the model from drifting too far from coherent language~\cite{schulman2017proximal}.
 
    \item \textbf{Iteration.} The process repeats: new outputs are collected, annotators provide fresh rankings, and both the reward model and the policy are updated.
\end{enumerate}
 
\begin{figure}[!h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=9cm, height=6cm]{images/rlhf.pdf}
    \caption{Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback pipeline.}
    \label{fig:rlhf_diagram}
\end{figure}
 
Figure~\ref{fig:rlhf_diagram} illustrates this pipeline. While effective, RLHF is expensive: it requires training and maintaining a separate reward model, collecting large volumes of human preference data, and managing the instability of RL training. These costs have motivated the development of simpler alignment methods such as Direct Preference Optimisation (DPO), which reformulates the RLHF objective to directly optimise on preference pairs without an explicit reward model~\cite{rafailov2023direct}.


\section{Prompting}
\label{sec:prompting}

While pre-training and fine-tuning adjust model parameters, \emph{prompting} shapes model behavior at inference time through instructions, context, and examples---without changing any weights. The discovery that including a few input--output examples in the prompt (\emph{few-shot prompting}) can elicit task-specific behavior from large decoder-only models without any fine-tuning opened the door to a set of techniques collectively known as \emph{prompt engineering} that exploit the model's in-context learning capabilities~\cite{brown2020languagemodelsfewshotlearners}.
 
\textbf{Task decomposition} breaks a complex problem into smaller sub-tasks, each with narrower scope and clearer success criteria. Rather than asking a model to solve a difficult problem in one step, the prompt guides it through a sequence of manageable steps whose outputs compose into a final answer. This reduces the cognitive burden on any single inference call and makes it easier to verify intermediate results.
 
\textbf{Chain-of-thought (CoT)} prompting is a specific form of task decomposition in which the model is encouraged to produce intermediate reasoning steps before arriving at a final answer. By including examples that show step-by-step reasoning, the prompt elicits similar behaviour from the model, which has been shown to improve performance on arithmetic, commonsense reasoning, and other multi-step tasks~\cite{wei2023chain}.
 
\textbf{Few-shot learning} calibrates what ``good'' output looks like by providing concrete examples in the prompt. The model uses these examples to infer the desired format, style, level of detail, and reasoning strategy, then applies these inferred patterns to the actual query. Few-shot prompting is particularly effective when combined with retrieval: instead of relying on fixed examples, relevant demonstrations can be retrieved dynamically from a knowledge base, tailoring the prompt to each specific input.
 
Together, these prompting techniques allow practitioners to adapt a single general-purpose model to a wide range of tasks at inference time, complementing the more permanent adaptations achieved through fine-tuning.

\section{Challenges and Limitations}
\label{sec:llm-challenges}
% ============================================================

Despite rapid progress, LLM-based smart contract auditing faces fundamental challenges that constrain its reliability in production settings.
 
 
\subsection*{Hallucinations}
 
LLMs can produce plausible-sounding but factually incorrect outputs ---~\emph{hallucination}. In code generation, this manifests as references to non-existent libraries, fabricated API calls, and invented vulnerability reports. Spracklen et al.~\cite{spracklen2025} tested 16~LLMs generating 576,000~code samples and found that 19.7\% of the 2.23~million referenced packages were hallucinated, creating a direct supply-chain attack vector.

% Pearce et al.~\cite{pearce2022asleep} found that approximately 40\% of GitHub Copilot's code completions contained vulnerabilities, while Perry et al.~\cite{perry2023users} demonstrated that users with AI coding assistant access wrote significantly less secure code while being more confident in its correctness---a dangerous combination of automation bias and misplaced trust.
 
% In the smart contract domain specifically, Chen et al.~\cite{chen2024chatgpt} identified several hallucination patterns: the model assumes protective code exists when it does not (``Protected Mechanism Bias''), assumes developers followed best practices (``Development Intent Bias''), and misinterprets Solidity-specific constructs like \texttt{msg.value}. The primary mitigation is to require \emph{grounded evidence} for every finding---each reported vulnerability should reference specific code locations, static analysis outputs, or retrieved documentation rather than relying on the model's unsupported assertions.
 
 
\subsection*{Context Window Limitations}
 
Even with context windows reaching hundreds of thousands of tokens, real smart contract systems can exceed what is reasonable to include in a single prompt. Imported libraries, interface definitions, deployment configurations, and cross-contract dependencies all consume the token budget. Moreover, models have a fixed training cutoff: new vulnerability classes and evolving best practices emerge continuously but are absent from the model's parametric knowledge. RAG partially addresses both issues by retrieving up-to-date, task-relevant information at inference time and by focusing the prompt on high-signal content~\cite{lewis2021}.
 
 
\subsection*{Security}
 
When LLM agents can call external tools---compilers, static analysers, blockchain RPCs---additional safety concerns arise. Prompt injection attacks embedded in retrieved documents or source code comments could manipulate the agent's behaviour, causing it to suppress genuine findings or fabricate false ones. Unrestricted tool access could enable unintended actions such as deploying contracts or initiating transactions. These risks require careful sandboxing of tool execution, input sanitisation of retrieved content, and human-in-the-loop confirmation for any actions with on-chain consequences.

\label{subsubsec:wake-ddg}

The DDG is one of Wake's most sophisticated analysis structures, tracking how data flows through the contract. It automatically expands composite types (structs, arrays, mappings) to track data flow at the member/element level. For example, when a struct is assigned, the DDG creates edges not only for the struct itself but also for each of its members recursively.

Smart contract auditing has evolved into a specialized discipline that combines elements of traditional software security review with domain-specific knowledge about blockchain systems and economic attack vectors. Auditors begin by establishing what the system is supposed to do and identifying potential threat vectors, then systematically examine the implementation through a combination of manual review and automated tools. While human expertise remains central to catching subtle logic bugs and business logic flaws, automation has become increasingly important for handling the scale and complexity of modern DeFi protocols \cite{perez2021smart}. 

\subsubsection*{System Overview}

Before any code is examined, the auditor establishes the scope of the 
engagement: which contracts are in scope, what external protocols they 
depend on, and what the system is designed to do. This typically produces 
a high-level map of contract interactions, entry points, and asset flows. 
Understanding the intended behavior is a prerequisite for high-qualitative and precise audit.

\subsubsection*{Trust Model}

The trust model defines which actors are assumed honest and which may be 
adversarial. In DeFi protocols this typically means treating all external 
users as potentially malicious while extending limited trust to governance 
multi-sigs or privileged roles. Alongside actor assumptions, the auditor 
documents state invariants that later serve as explicit targets for both manual verification and property-based fuzzing.

\subsubsection*{Manual Code Review}

Manual review remains indispensable because many vulnerabilities are not 
pattern matches, but failures of business logic that require understanding 
the intent of the programmer. A typical review proceeds in three phases:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Architectural analysis} - how contracts interact, what 
    external systems they depend on, and where trust boundaries lie.
    \item \textbf{Execution path tracing} - walking through critical user 
    interactions like deposits, withdrawals, liquidations and other, for purpose of looking for missing checks or incorrect ordering 
    of operations.

\subsubsection*{Tool-Based Static Analysis}

Static analysis tools examine code without executing it, flagging known 
vulnerability patterns across the entire codebase in seconds. Wake framework provides more than 50 static detectors for these purposes.

\subsubsection*{Local Deployment and Testing}

Testing smart contracts in realistic conditions requires deploying them 
to a local blockchain environment where behavior can be observed without 
risking real funds. Unit tests verify individual functions in isolation, 
while integration tests exercise complete workflows such as deposit, 
borrow, and liquidation sequences. A particularly powerful technique is 
fork testing: the local chain copies mainnet state at a specific block, 
allowing the contract under audit to interact with real deployed 
protocols---actual DEXes, oracles, and tokens---without requiring manual 
mocks. This also enables forensic reproduction of historical exploits by 
forking just before a known attack and replaying the attack transaction.

The limitation of any test suite is that coverage depends entirely on 
which scenarios the test author anticipated. Attackers deliberately seek 
the scenarios that were not anticipated, which motivates the use of 
automated input generation through fuzzing.

\subsubsection*{Fuzz Testing}

Fuzzing automatically generates large numbers of inputs to explore 
program behavior beyond what manually written tests cover. 
Property-based fuzzers such as Echidna and Foundry's fuzzer go further: 
the auditor specifies invariants that must hold under all conditions, and 
the fuzzer attempts to find any sequence of transactions that violates 
them. Stateful fuzzing maintains contract state across a sequence of 
calls, which is essential for catching vulnerabilities that only emerge 
through specific multi-step interactions---a class of bug that neither 
static analysis nor unit tests reliably detect.

The practical limitation is that the fuzzing is probabilistic rather than 
exhaustive. A bug requiring a highly specific state setup may never be 
triggered within a feasible run budget, and writing effective invariant 
specifications requires the same domain expertise as manual review. In 
practice, fuzzing therefore complements rather than replaces the earlier 
phases: static analysis provides broad fast coverage, manual review 
applies contextual reasoning, and fuzzing stress-tests the invariants 
that both preceding phases identified as critical.

\section{Design Goals}
\label{sec:design-goals}

The design of the RAG-augmented auditing system is guided by three goals, each motivated by the limitations identified in Sections~\ref{sec:llm-challenges} and~\ref{subsec:wake-ai-limitations}.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Groundedness.} Every claim in the LLM's output should be traceable to a concrete piece of evidence: a retrieved audit finding, a static-analysis result, or a specific code location. This directly mitigates the risk of hallucination by requiring the model to anchor its reasoning in documented vulnerabilities rather than relying on parametric memory alone.

    \item \textbf{Token efficiency.} LLM context windows are finite and costly. Rather than concatenating entire codebases or knowledge bases into the prompt, the system retrieves a small, highly relevant subset of documents and injects only the most informative content. This keeps inference fast, reduces cost, and avoids the noise degradation that occurs when irrelevant material is included~\cite{lewis2021retrieval}.

    \item \textbf{Audit usefulness.} The system must produce structured reports that include a verdict (vulnerable or safe), a confidence level, and a detailed reasoning trace that references specific past findings. This aligns with industry audit report conventions and allows auditors to use the output as a starting point for deeper investigation rather than a final answer.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Retrieval-Augmented Generation}
\label{sec:rag}

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) addresses two fundamental limitations of standalone language models: the inability to access information beyond the training data cutoff, and the tendency to hallucinate when answering questions that require specialized knowledge~\cite{lewis2021retrieval}. Rather than encoding all knowledge in model weights, RAG augments the generator with a retrieval component that fetches relevant documents from an external corpus at inference time. This separation has a practical consequence: the knowledge base can be updated, extended, or replaced without retraining the model.

In the context of smart contract auditing, RAG enables the system to leverage continuously growing databases of historical audit findings --- precisely the kind of specialized, evolving knowledge that language models struggle to maintain through pre-training alone.

\subsection{Formal Framework}
\label{subsec:rag-formal}

A RAG system comprises two components. The \emph{retriever} $p_\eta$ produces a distribution over text passages~$z$ given an input query~$x$:

\begin{equation}
    p_\eta(z \mid x),
    \label{eq:retriever}
\end{equation}

where $\eta$ denotes the retriever parameters --- in practice, the weights of an embedding model that maps both queries and passages into a shared vector space, so that relevance reduces to vector similarity.

The \emph{generator} $p_\theta$ produces each output token $y_i$ conditioned on the input~$x$, a retrieved passage~$z$, and all previously generated tokens $y_{1:i-1}$:

\begin{equation}
    p_\theta(y_i \mid x, z, y_{1:i-1}),
    \label{eq:generator}
\end{equation}

where $\theta$ denotes the generator parameters. In this work, the generator is a frontier LLM accessed through an API, and the retrieved passages are historical audit findings with similar vulnerability patterns.

The probability of the full output sequence is marginalized over the retrieved top passages $k$:

\begin{equation}
    p(y \mid x) \;\approx\; \sum_{z \in \text{top-}k}
    p_\eta(z \mid x)\;\prod_{i=1}^{N}
    p_\theta(y_i \mid x, z, y_{1:i-1}).
    \label{eq:rag-marginal}
\end{equation}

This formulation makes explicit the three design decisions that shape any RAG system: \emph{what} to store in the knowledge base (the corpus over which $z$ ranges), \emph{how} to retrieve (the retriever $p_\eta$ and the value of $k$), and \emph{how} to present the retrieved context to the generator (the prompt structure that conditions $p_\theta$). The following sections address each of these decisions in turn.


\section{Knowledge Base}
\label{sec:knowledge-base}

The effectiveness of a RAG system depends on two factors: the quality and coverage of its knowledge base, and the correctness with which the retrieved documents are utilized during generation. A knowledge base that is noisy, incomplete, or poorly structured degrades retrieval precision regardless of how sophisticated the retrieval mechanism is~\cite{manning2008irbook}.

\subsection{Data Source}
\label{subsec:solodit}

The knowledge base is constructed from Solodit\footnote{\url{https://solodit.cyfrin.io}}, a comprehensive aggregator of smart contract audit findings maintained by Cyfrin. Solodit indexes the findings of the major audit firms and competitive audit platforms, including Trail of Bits, OpenZeppelin, Spearbit, Code4rena, and Sherlock, among others. Each finding includes a title, detailed markdown content (with vulnerability description, proof of concept, recommended mitigation, and inline Solidity code), a severity classification, source metadata, and community-contributed tags.

Solodit was selected as the primary data source for three reasons. First, it aggregates findings across multiple audit firms, providing broader coverage than any single firm's reports. Second, each finding is a self-contained unit with structured metadata, which makes it suitable for embedding and retrieval without extensive reformatting. Third, its REST API enables incremental ingestion: the pipeline fetches findings sorted by recency, stopping upon encountering an already-ingested entry. This ensures that the knowledge base grows monotonically without duplication, keeping it up to date with recently disclosed vulnerabilities.

Ingestion is limited to findings classified as \texttt{MEDIUM}, \texttt{HIGH}, or \texttt{CRITICAL} severity. Lower-severity entries (gas optimizations, informational notes) were excluded because they dilute the semantic coherence of the embedding space without contributing to vulnerability detection.

\subsection{Data Preprocessing}
\label{subsec:preprocessing}

The raw audit findings vary widely in format, length, and writing style. Directly embedding unprocessed text leads to noisy vector representations where formatting artifacts such as markdown headers, code fences, HTML entities could dominate the embedding signal over the actual vulnerability semantics. The preprocessing pipeline therefore normalizes each finding into a uniform structure optimized for embedding.

\subsubsection*{Code Block Extraction}

Fenced code blocks are extracted using regular expression matching and stored separately in a dedicated JSONB column. This separation serves two purposes: it enables code-specific embedding using a specialized model, and it allows the textual summary to focus on the vulnerability description without code noise.

The extracted code blocks are normalized for embedding by flattening each block into a single line (replacing newlines and tabs with spaces, collapsing duplicate whitespace) and truncating to 5{,}000 characters. A single finding may yield multiple code blocks, each embedded independently. This explains why the number of code embeddings exceeds the number of findings in the knowledge base.

\subsubsection*{Summary Generation}

Each finding undergoes an LLM-based summarization (GPT-5.1, temperature~0) to produce three outputs: (i)~a human-readable summary of 5--10 sentences that capture the vulnerability description, (ii)~a dense, keyword-rich summary optimized for semantic search that includes the vulnerability type, affected components, attack vectors, and impact, and (iii)~a vulnerability label selected from a predefined taxonomy. The keyword-rich summary serves as the primary text for embedding, as it concentrates the most retrieval-relevant information into a compact representation.

\subsubsection*{Label Assignment}
\label{subsubsec:labeling}

The LLM assigns to each finding a vulnerability label from a predefined taxonomy of 20~categories, covering common smart contract vulnerability classes such as Access Control, Reentrancy, Logic Error, Fund Loss, and Denial of Service.

\subsection{Database Schema}
\label{subsec:db-schema}

Storing and querying vector embeddings at scale requires infrastructure that supports an efficient approximate nearest-neighbor (ANN) search. Purpose-built vector databases such as Pinecone~\cite{pinecone2024}, Weaviate~\cite{weaviate2024}, and Milvus~\cite{wang2021milvus} are designed specifically for this task: they offer managed indexing, automatic sharding, and optimized distance computations out of the box. However, these systems operate as standalone services, adding operational complexity for this thesis.

An alternative approach is to extend a general-purpose relational database with vector capabilities. The \textit{pgvector} extension~\cite{pgvector2023} adds native vector column types and distance operators to PostgreSQL, enabling the storage of embedding indexes, and similarity search within the same transactional database that already holds structured metadata. For this project, the key advantage is co-location: a single query can filter by relational attributes (e.g., embedding model, content mode, vulnerability label) and rank by vector similarity in one pass, without cross-service joins.

% \begin{figure}[!ht]
%     \centering
%     \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/erd.pdf}
%     \caption{Entity-relationship diagram of the database schema. The left group stores the knowledge base (findings, embeddings, chunks); the right group supports the evaluation pipeline (experiments, cases, results).}
%     \label{fig:erd}
% \end{figure}

The schema, illustrated in the entity-relationship diagram in Figure~\ref{fig:erd}, comprises six tables organized into two logical groups. The \emph{knowledge base} group consists of three tables: \texttt{findings} stores the core entries with their metadata, preprocessed content, and code blocks; \texttt{finding\_embeddings} stores vector embeddings linked to findings via a foreign key, supporting multiple embedding models and content modes (text or code) through dedicated columns; and \texttt{finding\_chunks} stores token-level chunks of findings for the chunked retrieval strategy. The \emph{evaluation} group contains: \texttt{evaluation\_cases}, which holds structured test inputs with ground-truth labels and reference answers; \texttt{experiments}, which records each evaluation run together with a frozen configuration snapshot and git commit hash for reproducibility; and \texttt{evaluation\_results}, which links individual metric scores back to their experiment and case.

\paragraph{HNSW indexes.}
The exact nearest-neighbor search over vectors requires computing the distance to every row in the table --- a linear scan that becomes prohibitively slow as the dataset grows. The approximate nearest-neighbor indexes trade a small amount of recall for dramatically lower query latency. Among the available ANN algorithms, Hierarchical Navigable Small World (HNSW) graphs~\cite{malkov2020hnsw} offer a strong balance between recall, speed, and incremental insertability. HNSW constructs a multi-layer graph in which each layer is a navigable small-world network: the top layers contain few, widely connected nodes for coarse navigation, while the bottom layer connects all nodes for fine-grained search. A query traverses the graph greedily from the top layer downward, narrowing the candidate set at each level.

\paragraph{Search functions.}
Four PostgreSQL functions encapsulate the similarity search logic. The text and code search functions each accept a query vector, a result count~$k$, a similarity threshold, and an optional label filter. The similarity is computed as $1 - d_{\cos}$ using pgvector's \texttt{<=>} operator. The functions join the embedding and finding tables, filter by model and mode, apply the threshold, and return ranked results with full finding metadata. Encapsulating this logic in database functions keeps the search semantics version-controlled alongside the schema and avoids duplicating filtering logic across application code.

\subsection{Embeddings}
\label{subsec:indexing-embeddings}

Each finding is embedded independently along two modalities, reflecting the dual nature of audit findings: they contain both natural-language vulnerability summary and Solidity code snippets.

\subsubsection*{Text Embeddings}

The textual summary is embedded using \textbf{nomic-embed-text-v1.5}~\cite{nussbaum2024nomic}, a 768-dimensional model. It uses task-specific prefixes: documents are prefixed with \texttt{"search\_document: "} during indexing, and queries with \texttt{"search\_query: "} during retrieval. All embeddings are L2-normalized, converting cosine similarity into inner product for efficient pgvector search.

\subsubsection*{Code Embeddings}

Code blocks are embedded using \textbf{CodeRankEmbed}~\cite{suresh2025rank}, a 768-dimensional model trained specifically for code search. It uses the prefix \texttt{"Represent this query for searching relevant code: "} for query embedding. Code embeddings enable retrieval where the query is a Solidity code snippet, directly matching structural code patterns across findings.

This dual-modality approach allows the retrieval pipeline to match queries against both the semantic description and the actual code of historical findings. The choice between text and code retrieval or a combination of both. This evaluation of configurations is explored in the Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation}.


\section{Retrieval Pipeline}
\label{sec:retrieval}

The retrieval pipeline transforms a query into a ranked list of the $k$ most relevant historical findings. It consists of two stages: an initial dense vector search that identifies candidate findings by embedding similarity, followed by an optional re-ranking step that refines the candidate ordering using a more expensive cross-attention model.

\subsection{Dense Vector Search}
\label{subsec:vector-search}

For dense retrieval, the query is embedded using the appropriate model and compared against stored embeddings using cosine similarity computed inside PostgreSQL. Depending on the search mode, the system invokes the corresponding database function:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Text search} retrieves findings by comparing against text embeddings generated from preprocessed summaries.
    \item \textbf{Code search} retrieves findings by comparing against code embeddings generated from extracted code blocks.
    \item \textbf{Hybrid search} (text + code) performs both searches independently, then merges results by finding identifier, keeping the higher similarity score when a finding appears in both result sets.
\end{itemize}

The hybrid mode exploits the complementary nature of the two modalities: text embeddings capture vulnerability semantics (e.g., ``reentrancy in token transfer''), while code embeddings capture structural patterns (e.g., an external call followed by a state update).

\subsection{Re-ranking}
\label{subsec:reranking}

Initial dense retrieval optimizes for recall: it casts a wide net using embedding similarity, which is fast but may rank marginally relevant findings above highly relevant ones. The fundamental limitation is that bi-encoder models embed the query and each document \emph{independently},so the similarity score cannot capture fine-grained token-level interactions between them. A cross-encoder~\cite{nogueira2019passage} overcomes this by processing the query and document as a single concatenated input:
%
\begin{equation}
    s(q, d) = \sigma\,\bigl(W^\top \mathbf{h}_{\texttt{[CLS]}}(q \oplus d)\bigr),
    \label{eq:cross-encoder}
\end{equation}
%
where $q \oplus d$ denotes the concatenation of the query and document tokens, $\mathbf{h}_{\texttt{[CLS]}}$ is the final hidden state of the \texttt{[CLS]} token, and $\sigma$ is the sigmoid function. Because attention flows across both inputs jointly, the model can detect relevance signals that depend on the specific \emph{combination} of query and document content. The cost is quadratic in sequence length, making cross-encoders impractical for first-stage retrieval over the full corpus but well-suited for re-scoring a small candidate set.

The system implements re-ranking through a cross-encoder model. Each candidate's content (truncated to 4{,}096 characters) is paired with the query and scored via Equation~\ref{eq:cross-encoder} in a single batch. Candidates are re-sorted by descending score and the top~$k$ are retained. For ablation purposes, re-ranking can be disabled entirely, in which case the retriever's original ranking is preserved and simply truncated to~$k$.

The specific re-ranker models and their comparative performance are evaluated in Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation}.


\section{Model Context Protocol Integration}
\label{sec:mcp-integration}

The Model Context Protocol (MCP)~\cite{anthropic2024mcp} is an open standard for connecting AI models with external tools and data sources. It defines a structured interface through which an LLM-based agent can discover available tools, invoke them with typed inputs, and receive structured outputs. Wake-AI already uses MCP to expose its static analysis capabilities (IR queries, CFG traversal, detector invocation) to the LLM agent.

\subsection{RAG Tools}
\label{subsec:mcp-wake}

The RAG system extends Wake-AI's MCP surface with two additional tools for knowledge base interaction:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Search tool.} Accepts a natural-language description of a suspected vulnerability (and optional parameters: result count~$k$, similarity threshold, label filter, search mode) and returns a ranked list of similar historical findings with full metadata including title, summary, label, severity, code blocks, and similarity score.

    \item \textbf{Retrieve details tool.} Given a finding identifier, returns the complete finding content, enabling the agent to request full details after an initial search returned only summaries.
\end{itemize}

This design allows the LLM agent to iteratively refine its retrieval: it might first search broadly for ``reentrancy in ERC-20 transfer'', inspect the returned summaries, then retrieve full details for the most promising matches. The iterative retrieval pattern is more token-efficient than pre-loading all potentially relevant documents into the prompt.

\subsection{Proactive RAG}
\label{subsec:proactive-rag}

A distinctive feature of the integration is the \emph{proactive} retrieval strategy embedded in the discovery prompt. Rather than waiting for the LLM to encounter a suspected vulnerability before querying the knowledge base, the prompt instructs the model to search proactively after analysing each non-trivial function.

The discovery prompt structures the audit as a systematic, function-by-function analysis. The LLM first lists all functions in the target contract, then analyses each one against a security checklist covering input validation, access control, front-running, denial of service, state change ordering, and other common vulnerability classes. After completing this checklist for each function, the following instruction triggers a knowledge base query:

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=gray!5, colframe=black!60, fonttitle=\bfseries, title=Prompt: Proactive RAG Instruction]
After analyzing each non-trivial function (more than \textasciitilde5 lines of logic, performs state changes, sends ETH, makes external calls, or handles signatures/proofs), also call \texttt{get\_similar\_issues} with a description of \textbf{what the function does, its key behaviors and possible issue} --- even if you haven't found a vulnerability yet. Skip trivial getters, pure math helpers, and simple event emitters.
\end{tcolorbox}

The proactive strategy is motivated by a key observation: an auditor may not recognise a vulnerability pattern from code inspection alone, but a semantically similar historical finding could reveal the risk. By searching the knowledge base \emph{before} forming a verdict, the LLM gains access to documented precedents that might otherwise be missed. The knowledge base returns the top results using hybrid retrieval (dense vector search combined with BM25 keyword matching), providing the LLM with historical findings that share both semantic similarity and specific technical terminology with the function under review.

\subsection{Security Considerations}
\label{subsec:mcp-security}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Read-only surface.} All MCP tools are strictly read-only: they query the knowledge base but cannot modify it.

    \item \textbf{Input sanitisation.} Query strings are used as embedding inputs and BM25 tokenisation inputs. Neither pathway evaluates queries as code or SQL. The pgvector search functions use parameterised queries, preventing SQL injection.

    \item \textbf{Empty results.} The system returns an explicit ``no relevant findings found'' message rather than an empty response, preventing the LLM from hallucinating findings when retrieval yields no matches.

    \item \textbf{Deterministic logging.} All MCP tool invocations, inputs, and outputs are logged with timestamps for post-hoc auditing and reproducibility.
\end{itemize}

\bigskip

This chapter presented the design of a RAG-augmented smart contract auditing methodology driven by three goals: groundedness, token efficiency, and audit usefulness. The system constructs a knowledge base from historical audit findings sourced from Solodit, embeds them along two modalities (text and code), and stores the resulting vectors in PostgreSQL with pgvector. At analysis time, a dense vector retrieval pipeline surfaces the most relevant historical findings, with an optional cross-encoder re-ranking stage to improve precision. The retrieval capability is integrated into Wake-AI's auditing workflow through the Model Context Protocol, using a proactive retrieval strategy that queries the knowledge base after each function analysis---even before a vulnerability is suspected.

\section{Research Questions}
\label{sec:research-questions}

The evaluation is structured around five research questions:

\begin{description}
    \item[RQ1: Retrieval effectiveness.] How effectively does the retrieval pipeline surface relevant historical findings for a given vulnerability query? Five query strategies are compared---description-based, code-based, description with label, hybrid BM25, and chunked retrieval---each with and without cross-encoder re-ranking.

    \item[RQ2: RAG vs.\ LLM-only detection.] Does augmenting the LLM with retrieved findings improve its ability to correctly classify vulnerabilities compared to using the LLM alone? This is measured by running the same set of real-world findings through both a RAG-augmented pipeline and an LLM-only baseline.

    \item[RQ3: Groundedness and reasoning quality.] Are the LLM's explanations faithful to the retrieved evidence? This question evaluates whether RAG reduces hallucinated vulnerability reports by measuring faithfulness and context utilisation scores.

    \item[RQ4: Component contributions.] What is the individual contribution of each pipeline component---re-ranking, BM25 fusion, chunked retrieval? Ablation experiments isolate each component's effect on retrieval and generation quality.

    \item[RQ5: Real-world applicability.] Can the system discover real vulnerabilities from full smart contracts in a realistic audit scenario? This is evaluated through whole-contract review on a curated dataset and through integration with the Wake static analyser.
\end{description}


\section{Datasets}
\label{sec:dataset}

Three datasets are used, each targeting a different aspect of the system.

\subsection{Knowledge Base}
\label{subsec:kb-corpus}

The knowledge base described in Section~\ref{sec:knowledge-base} contains 16{,}780 security audit findings ingested from the Solodit API, filtered to \texttt{HIGH} and \texttt{MEDIUM} severity. The preprocessing pipeline produced three types of embeddings:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Text embeddings:} 16{,}780 vectors from preprocessed keyword-rich summaries (nomic-embed-text-v1.5, 768-dimensional).
    \item \textbf{Code embeddings:} 27{,}222 vectors from individual extracted code blocks (CodeRankEmbed, 768-dimensional).
    \item \textbf{Chunk embeddings:} 152{,}869 vectors from overlapping 512-character windows over the raw finding content (nomic-embed-text-v1.5, 768-dimensional), with 128-character overlap producing approximately 9~chunks per finding.
\end{itemize}

The chunk embeddings provide a fine-grained alternative to summary-level embeddings: rather than matching against a single summary vector per finding, chunked retrieval matches against multiple overlapping windows of the original text, potentially capturing details lost during summarisation.

\subsection{RAG Evaluation Dataset}
\label{subsec:eval-cases}

No established benchmark exists for evaluating RAG-augmented smart contract vulnerability detection. To address this gap, 40~evaluation cases were manually constructed to cover a representative distribution of vulnerability types and difficulty levels. Each case consists of:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Solidity code} --- a function-level code snippet that demonstrates (or does not exhibit) a specific vulnerability.
    \item \textbf{Issue description} --- a natural-language description of the suspected vulnerability.
    \item \textbf{Label} --- the vulnerability category from the predefined taxonomy.
    \item \textbf{Expected verdict} --- ground-truth classification: \texttt{VULNERABLE} (28 cases) or \texttt{SAFE} (12 cases).
    \item \textbf{Reference answer} --- an expert-written explanation for answer correctness evaluation.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Golden Dataset}
\label{subsec:golden-dataset}

A curated dataset of 40~real-world vulnerability findings drawn from actual security audits serves as the most realistic evaluation benchmark. Unlike the RAG evaluation cases, which are synthetic, these are genuine findings from projects audited on platforms aggregated by Solodit. Each entry includes the complete Solidity source file, an expert-written issue description, and relevant metadata.

All 40~golden findings have \texttt{expected\_verdict = VULNERABLE}. The dataset is used for two evaluation types:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Golden retrieval.} The LLM reviews the \emph{entire} smart contract (not an isolated function) and identifies suspected vulnerable functions with short descriptions. These descriptions then serve as retrieval queries. An LLM judge verifies whether the system-identified issue matches the known golden vulnerability. This simulates a realistic audit workflow where the auditor does not know in advance which function is vulnerable.

    \item \textbf{Golden generation.} The full RAG pipeline is executed for each finding. Crucially, the finding itself is excluded from the retrieved context to prevent trivial self-matching. A separate baseline runs the LLM without any retrieval, enabling a direct comparison of RAG-augmented versus LLM-only detection.
\end{itemize}


\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:experimental-setup}

\subsection{Infrastructure}
\label{subsec:infrastructure}

All experiments run on a single machine with Apple Silicon (MPS backend for local models). PostgreSQL~17 with pgvector serves as both knowledge base and experiment store. The LLM (GPT-5.1, temperature~0) is accessed via the OpenAI API. The local models used throughout the experiments are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Text embedding:} nomic-ai/nomic-embed-text-v1.5 (768-dimensional, query prefix: \texttt{"search\_query: "}).
    \item \textbf{Code embedding:} nomic-ai/CodeRankEmbed (768-dimensional, query prefix: \texttt{"Represent this query for searching relevant code: "}).
    \item \textbf{Cross-encoder re-ranker:} cross-encoder/ms-marco-MiniLM-L-6-v2 (22M parameters).
\end{itemize}


\subsection{Query Strategies}
\label{subsec:query-strategies}

Five retrieval strategies are evaluated, each varying the query construction, embedding space, or retrieval mechanism. The dense vector search and re-ranking components described in Section~\ref{sec:retrieval} form the backbone; the strategies differ in how the query is formulated and what additional retrieval mechanisms are combined.

\begin{table}[!ht]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{llp{5.5cm}}
\toprule
\textbf{Strategy} & \textbf{Query source} & \textbf{What it tests} \\
\midrule
\texttt{description} & Issue description & Semantic similarity from natural-language description \\
\texttt{code} & Solidity code & Structural code pattern similarity \\
\texttt{description\_label} & Description + label & Category-enriched semantic search \\
\texttt{hybrid\_bm25} & Description & Dense vector + keyword fusion via RRF \\
\texttt{chunked} & Description & Fine-grained matching against raw content windows \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Retrieval query strategies evaluated in the experiments (all use 768-dimensional embeddings, $k{=}5$).}
\label{tab:query-strategies}
\end{table}

Table~\ref{tab:query-strategies} summarises the five strategies. The first three use the dense vector search pipeline directly, varying only the query input. The remaining two introduce additional retrieval mechanisms:

\paragraph{Hybrid BM25.} This strategy combines dense vector retrieval with BM25 keyword search~\cite{robertson2009bm25}. BM25 compensates for a known weakness of dense retrieval: semantic embedding models may miss findings that use the exact Solidity identifier mentioned in the query. For example, a query about \texttt{transferFrom} reentrancy should retrieve findings mentioning \texttt{transferFrom} by name, even if their embeddings emphasise different semantic aspects.

The BM25 index is built over all finding summaries using the BM25Okapi variant. Tokenisation preserves domain-specific identifiers (\texttt{delegatecall}, \texttt{msg.sender}, \texttt{ERC20}) by splitting on non-alphanumeric characters and retaining multi-character tokens. The two ranked lists (dense and BM25) are merged using Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF)~\cite{cormack2009rrf}:

\begin{equation}
    \text{RRF}(d) = \sum_{i=1}^{L} \frac{1}{k_{\text{rrf}} + r_i(d)},
    \label{eq:rrf}
\end{equation}

where $L$ is the number of ranked lists, $r_i(d)$ is the rank of document~$d$ in list~$i$ (1-indexed), and $k_{\text{rrf}} = 60$ (the default from the original paper). Documents not appearing in a given list are omitted from the corresponding term. Both sources contribute 50 candidates each before fusion.

\paragraph{Chunked retrieval.} Instead of matching against a single summary embedding per finding, this strategy matches against overlapping 512-character windows of the raw finding content. If any chunk of a finding matches the query, the entire finding is returned with the maximum chunk similarity as its score. This approach can surface findings where the relevant detail appears in a specific paragraph rather than being captured by the summary.


\subsection{Evaluation Prompts}
\label{subsec:eval-prompts}

Several metrics rely on an LLM judge (GPT-5.1, temperature~0) to assess retrieval relevance and generation quality. The use of LLM-based evaluation has been shown to correlate well with human judgement for text quality assessment tasks~\cite{zheng2023judging}. Two judge prompts are used for retrieval evaluation:

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=gray!5, colframe=black!60, fonttitle=\bfseries, title=Prompt 1: Context Relevance (Holistic)]
You are a senior smart contract security auditor evaluating whether retrieved past audit findings are relevant to a vulnerability being analysed. A finding is relevant when it describes the same or closely related vulnerability pattern, targets a similar Solidity construct, shares the same root cause or exploitation mechanism, or provides a directly applicable mitigation.
\end{tcolorbox}

The holistic judge receives the complete set of retrieved findings and scores overall relevance on a 0--1 scale, additionally providing structured feedback on coverage (\texttt{full}/\texttt{partial}/\texttt{none}) and specificity (\texttt{high}/\texttt{medium}/\texttt{low}).

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=gray!5, colframe=black!60, fonttitle=\bfseries, title=Prompt 2: Context Precision (Per-Finding)]
Decide whether the FINDING is relevant to the QUERY vulnerability. RELEVANT (verdict=1) if: same vulnerability class, same root cause, similar construct, would help an auditor. NOT RELEVANT (verdict=0) if: different vulnerability type, unrelated contract pattern, tangentially related.
\end{tcolorbox}

The per-finding judge evaluates each retrieved finding independently for binary relevance. This requires $k$~LLM calls per evaluation case but provides fine-grained diagnostics, identifying which specific findings in the retrieved set are useful and which are noise.


\section{Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}

\subsection{Retrieval Metrics}
\label{subsec:retrieval-metrics}

\subsubsection*{Deterministic Metrics}

When evaluation cases include ground-truth relevant finding identifiers, the following standard information retrieval metrics are computed:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Context Precision.} Fraction of retrieved findings that belong to the ground-truth relevant set:
    \begin{equation}
        \text{Precision} = \frac{|\text{retrieved} \cap \text{relevant}|}{|\text{retrieved}|}
    \end{equation}

    \item \textbf{Context Recall.} Fraction of ground-truth relevant findings that were retrieved:
    \begin{equation}
        \text{Recall} = \frac{|\text{retrieved} \cap \text{relevant}|}{|\text{relevant}|}
    \end{equation}

    \item \textbf{MAP@$k$ (Mean Average Precision).} Rewards relevant items appearing at earlier rank positions:
    \begin{equation}
        \text{AP@}k = \frac{1}{\min(|\text{relevant}|, k)} \sum_{i=1}^{k} \text{Precision@}i \cdot \text{rel}(i)
    \end{equation}
    where $\text{rel}(i) = 1$ if the $i$-th result is relevant, 0 otherwise.

    \item \textbf{Recall@$k$.} Recall computed at a specific cutoff position:
    \begin{equation}
        \text{Recall@}k = \frac{|\text{relevant} \cap \text{retrieved}_{1:k}|}{|\text{relevant}|}
    \end{equation}

    \item \textbf{Label Match Ratio.} Fraction of retrieved findings whose vulnerability label matches the query label:
    \begin{equation}
        \text{LabelMatch} = \frac{|\{r \in \text{retrieved} : \text{label}(r) = \text{label}(q)\}|}{|\text{retrieved}|}
    \end{equation}
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{LLM-Judged Retrieval Metrics}

These metrics do not require ground-truth annotations and can evaluate retrieval quality on any query:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Context Relevance (holistic).} The LLM judge evaluates the entire retrieved set, scoring overall relevance on a 0--1 scale with structured coverage and specificity feedback.

    \item \textbf{Context Precision (per-finding).} Each retrieved finding is independently evaluated for binary relevance. The metric is the fraction of individually relevant findings.

    \item \textbf{Hit Rate@$k$.} Binary indicator: did the target finding appear in the top-$k$ results?

    \item \textbf{MRR (Mean Reciprocal Rank).} Averaged over all queries $Q$: $\text{MRR} = \frac{1}{|Q|}\sum_{q \in Q} \frac{1}{\text{rank}(q)}$, where $\text{rank}(q)$ is the position of the target finding.

    \item \textbf{NDCG@$k$.} For a single relevant item: $\frac{1}{\log_2(\text{rank} + 1)}$.

    \item \textbf{Label Hit Rate.} Fraction of queries where at least one retrieved finding shares the target's vulnerability label.

    \item \textbf{LLM Match Rate.} Fraction of golden vulnerabilities independently identified by the LLM during whole-contract review, before retrieval is performed.
\end{itemize}


\subsection{Generation Metrics}
\label{subsec:generation-metrics}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Verdict Accuracy} (deterministic). Binary match between the predicted verdict and the expected verdict. No LLM judge is required.

    \item \textbf{Faithfulness} (LLM-judged). The judge counts factual claims in the generated analysis and determines how many are supported by the retrieved context, producing a groundedness ratio on a 0--1 scale.

    \item \textbf{Reasoning Quality} (LLM-judged). Evaluates how well the analysis references past findings, draws accurate parallels between historical and current code, and supports its verdict with evidence. Scored 0--1 with an evidence usage assessment (\texttt{strong}/\texttt{adequate}/\texttt{weak}/\texttt{none}).

    \item \textbf{Context Utilisation} (LLM-judged). Evaluates whether the analysis explicitly cites retrieved findings, draws parallels between past and current code, and logically derives the verdict from the provided evidence.

    \item \textbf{Answer Correctness} (LLM-judged). Compares the generated analysis against a reference answer on three criteria: same verdict, same root cause identified, and technically accurate explanation. Available only when reference answers exist.
\end{itemize}

\section{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:threats}

\subsection{Internal Validity}

Several factors may affect the reliability of the measured results. The LLM-as-judge approach, while shown to correlate with human judgement~\cite{zheng2023judging}, may exhibit systematic biases---for example, favouring verbose explanations or penalising unconventional but correct reasoning. Setting the temperature to zero does not guarantee deterministic outputs from large language models; repeated runs may yield slightly different scores, introducing measurement noise.

The hand-crafted evaluation dataset (40~cases) is small and may not represent the full distribution of smart contract vulnerabilities encountered in practice. The summary generation step uses the same LLM family (GPT-5.1) as the evaluation judge, which could introduce a circular bias where the judge favours summaries generated by a similar model.

\subsection{External Validity}

The generalisability of the results is constrained by several design choices. The knowledge base draws from a single source (Solodit); findings from other aggregators or private audit databases may have different characteristics. All experiments use a single LLM (GPT-5.1); results may differ with other models. The embedding model pair (nomic-embed-text-v1.5 and CodeRankEmbed) was selected based on availability and dimension compatibility---other embedding models may yield different retrieval quality. Finally, all evaluation is limited to Solidity smart contracts; other smart contract languages such as Vyper or Move are not tested.

\subsection{Construct Validity}

Verdict accuracy on the golden dataset conflates two distinct capabilities: the ability to identify which function is vulnerable (a code comprehension task) and the ability to classify the vulnerability correctly (a security reasoning task). A system that finds the right function but misclassifies the vulnerability type receives the same zero score as one that analyses the wrong function entirely.

LLM-judged metrics are sensitive to prompt wording. The judge prompts used in this work (Section~\ref{subsec:eval-prompts}) were designed to elicit consistent scoring, but different formulations might produce different score distributions. The vulnerability label taxonomy, while comprehensive, may not capture all vulnerability types encountered in practice, and the LLM's label assignment introduces a subjective element into the Label Match Ratio metric.


\section{Summary}
\label{sec:eval-summary}

This chapter evaluated the RAG-augmented auditing system through four experiment suites, addressing the five research questions:

\begin{description}
    \item[RQ1 (Retrieval effectiveness):] The hybrid+BM25 strategy achieves the best retrieval quality (context precision 0.568, context relevance 0.805), confirming that combining dense semantic search with keyword matching captures both conceptual and terminological similarity. Cross-encoder re-ranking does not consistently improve results, likely due to domain mismatch between the re-ranker's training data and smart contract security.

    \item[RQ2 (RAG vs.\ LLM-only):] RAG improves faithfulness by 28~percentage points (0.641~$\to$~0.821), demonstrating that retrieved context grounds the LLM's analysis in documented evidence. Verdict accuracy on whole-contract review is low for both modes, reflecting the inherent difficulty of the task rather than a limitation of the RAG approach.

    \item[RQ3 (Groundedness):] The full pipeline achieves faithfulness of 0.765 and context utilisation of 0.851 on the evaluation dataset, confirming that the LLM actively incorporates retrieved findings into its analysis. Reasoning quality (0.908) is the strongest metric, indicating well-structured analytical output.

    \item[RQ4 (Component contributions):] BM25 fusion provides the largest single improvement over pure dense retrieval. Re-ranking is not beneficial in its current form. Chunked retrieval performs comparably to summary-based retrieval but does not surpass the hybrid+BM25 strategy.

    \item[RQ5 (Real-world applicability):] Integration with Wake-AI shows no statistically significant improvement in detection count (22/47~$\to$~23/47). The value of RAG integration lies in improved reasoning quality and groundedness rather than raw detection volume.
\end{description}

The results indicate that RAG augmentation primarily improves the \emph{quality} of the LLM's reasoning---making it more grounded, transparent, and verifiable---rather than increasing the \emph{quantity} of detected vulnerabilities. For a professional audit workflow, this qualitative improvement is arguably more valuable than marginal detection gains: an auditor benefits more from a well-grounded analysis that can be verified against documented precedents than from an additional detection that may itself require extensive verification.

\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{ch:conclusion}

In this thesis, we proposed a RAG-augmented system for LLM-based smart contract security auditing, integrated into the Wake framework through the MCP. The system addresses the knowledge cutoff limitation of large language models by grounding their reasoning in a continuously updated knowledge base of historical audit findings. Differently from prior work that relies on fine-tuning domain-specific models or prompt-only approaches, we proposed a retrieval-augmented architecture that separates knowledge acquisition from reasoning, allowing the knowledge base to grow independently without retraining the underlying model.

The presented solution comprises several concrete contributions. A domain-specific knowledge base was constructed from 16,780 real-world audit findings, preprocessed through LLM-based summarization and embedded along two modalities — text summaries and extracted Solidity code blocks. A retrieval pipeline was designed and evaluated across five query strategies, with the hybrid dense vector plus BM25 keyword fusion achieving the best performance. The retrieval capability was integrated into Wake-AI's auditing workflow via MCP, using a proactive strategy that queries the knowledge base after each function analysis even before a vulnerability is suspected.

As seen in the results, the RAG augmentation primarily improves the quality of the LLM's reasoning rather than the raw quantity of detected vulnerabilities. The most significant finding is that RAG improves faithfulness by 28 percentage points compared to the LLM-only baseline, demonstrating that retrieved historical findings effectively ground the model's analysis in documented evidence and reduce hallucinated claims. The retrieval pipeline achieves a Hit Rate@5 of 0.900 on real-world findings, and the LLM actively incorporates retrieved context into well-structured analyses, as confirmed by high reasoning quality and context utilization scores. A key finding is that hybrid retrieval combining dense vectors with BM25 keyword matching consistently outperforms pure dense retrieval, while cross-encoder re-ranking does not transfer well from its web search training domain.

However, several aspects remain open for future work. The knowledge base currently draws from a single open-source; expanding it to include private audit databases and bug bounty reports would increase coverage and diversity. Training a domain-specific cross-encoder on smart contract audit data would likely improve re-ranking over the current general-purpose model. Tighter integration with Wake's static analysis infrastructure could enable IR-guided retrieval, where queries are enriched with control flow and data dependency information rather than relying solely on natural-language descriptions. Finally, a standardized evaluation benchmark for RAG-augmented smart contract auditing would enable more rigorous cross-system comparison.
